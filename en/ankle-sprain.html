Condition guide

Ankle sprain

The ankle usually rolls inwards, straining or tearing the ligaments on the outer side. Most sprains heal without surgery. Early, gradual weight-bearing, exercise and balance training support recovery and also stop the same ankle from being sprained again.

What is it?

When the ankle turns inwards suddenly and forcefully, the ligaments in front of and below the outer ankle bone are stretched or torn. This causes pain, swelling and bruising, and it becomes hard to put weight on the foot.

If rehabilitation is left unfinished after a sprain, the ankle can become prone to spraining again, and some people develop a frequent feeling of the ankle giving way (chronic instability).

In the first few days

  • Support your ankle with tape or a brace, and put weight on it as far as the pain allows.
  • Use crutches for the first few days if you need to, but gradually put weight on the foot.
  • Using ice on and off and raising your leg help reduce swelling.
  • Keep the ankle moving from early on with ankle pumps.
  • If you need pain relief, ask your doctor.

Do you need an X-ray?

Most sprains do not involve a fracture and do not need an X-ray. The Ottawa ankle rules were developed to identify which patients need an X-ray, and in a review that combined 27 studies, they caught 97.6% of fractures.

You need an X-ray if there is pain around the ankle together with any of the following: bone tenderness along the back edge of the lower 6 cm, or at the tip, of the inner or outer ankle bone; or being unable to take 4 steps both straight after the injury and at the examination.

Treatment

The American Physical Therapy Association's 2021 guideline groups treatment after a sprain under the following headings.

  • Protection and gradual weight-bearingProtecting the ankle with external support, such as tape or a brace, and gradually putting weight on the foot are recommended.
  • ExerciseA structured programme is recommended, including range-of-motion, stretching, neuromuscular control, posture and balance exercises.
  • Manual therapyLymphatic drainage and soft tissue and joint mobilisation are carried out within a pain-free range.
  • Ice and medicationAlongside exercise, ice used on and off and, on your doctor's advice, anti-inflammatory painkillers can be used.
  • Returning to work and sportWork- and sport-specific training is planned from the early stages of rehabilitation; a brace can be worn when you return.
  • Preventing further sprainsFor people who have had a sprain, wearing a brace and following a balance-focused exercise programme are recommended.

Exercises at home

Six exercises for your ankle

You can start the first two exercises early on. As pain and swelling ease, move on to the strengthening and balance exercises. For exercises done standing, hold on to something sturdy; stop if you feel sharp pain.

Ankle pumps and circles

Sit or lie down with your leg straight. Pull your toes up towards you, then push them down; then draw circles with your ankle in both directions. From the first days, this helps reduce swelling and keep the ankle moving.

10 reps in each direction, 3–4 times a day

Towel calf stretch

Sit with your legs straight, loop a towel around the front of your foot and hold the ends. Keeping your knee straight, pull the towel towards you; feel a stretch in your calf. Stay within a pain-free range.

30 seconds, 3 reps

Resistance band eversion

Sit down, loop a resistance band around the front of your foot and fix the other end on the opposite side. Keeping your heel on the floor, turn your foot outwards, then slowly bring it back. This strengthens the muscles on the outer side of the ankle.

10–15 reps, 2–3 sets

Heel raises

Holding on to the counter, rise up onto your toes on both feet, then lower slowly. As it gets easier, do it on your sprained foot only.

10–15 reps, 2–3 sets

Standing on one leg

Stand close to the counter and balance on your sprained foot. As it gets easier, let go of the support, close your eyes or try it on a soft surface such as a cushion. This is the key exercise for preventing further sprains.

30 seconds, 3–5 reps

Heel-to-toe walking

Along the counter or a wall, walk in a straight line, placing the heel of your front foot just in front of the toes of your back foot.

10–15 steps, 2–3 rounds

Returning to sport and daily life

The pain going away does not mean your ankle has fully recovered. Returning gradually prevents further sprains.

  • Walk on flat ground first, then move on to light jogging, and finally to changes of direction and jumping.
  • Do not start running until you can balance comfortably on your sprained foot.
  • When you return to sport, use a brace or tape for support for a while.
  • Even after the pain has gone, keep doing balance exercises a few times a week.
  • Take care on uneven ground and in the dark; wear shoes that support the heel.

Frequently asked questions

Should I put weight on my sprained foot?

Yes, as far as the pain allows. The American Physical Therapy Association's guideline recommends protecting a new sprain with support such as tape or a brace, and gradually putting weight on the foot. Keeping all weight off it for a long time can slow recovery.

Do I need to have an X-ray?

For most sprains, no. According to the Ottawa rules, you need an X-ray if you cannot take 4 steps both straight after the injury and at the examination, or if there is bone tenderness along the back edge of the lower 6 cm of the inner or outer ankle bone. These rules catch almost all fractures.

Should I use ice?

Used alongside an exercise programme, repeated ice applied on and off is one of the methods the guideline lists for reducing pain and swelling. Do not put ice directly on your skin; place a thin cloth in between.

Should I wear an ankle brace?

Using support after a new sprain is recommended. For people who have had a sprain, wearing a brace during sport and doing balance exercises are also recommended to prevent further sprains.

Why do I keep spraining the same ankle?

If your balance, muscle strength and sense of where your ankle is have not fully recovered after a sprain, the ankle becomes prone to spraining again. The way to break this cycle is to keep doing balance and strengthening exercises even after the pain has gone, and to return to sport gradually.

When to seek help right away

See a doctor without delay if you have any of the following:

  • Being unable to take 4 steps straight after the injury and afterwards, or marked tenderness over the bone (possible fracture)
  • Visible deformity of the ankle
  • Pain at the back of the heel that started with a "pop", and being unable to rise onto your toes (possible Achilles tendon rupture)
  • Numbness, coldness or a change of colour in the foot
  • Swelling, pain and redness in the calf (possible blood clot); fever and redness that keeps spreading
