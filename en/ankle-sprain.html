Ankle sprain

The ankle usually rolls inwards, straining or tearing the ligaments on the outer side. Most sprains heal without surgery. Early, gradual weight-bearing and exercise support recovery; balance exercises and an ankle brace reduce the risk of the same ankle being sprained again.

What is it?

When the ankle twists inwards suddenly and forcefully, the ligaments in front of and below the outer ankle bone (malleolus) are stretched or torn. There is pain, swelling and bruising, and putting weight on the foot becomes difficult.

If rehabilitation is left unfinished after a sprain, the ankle can become prone to spraining again, and some people develop a frequent feeling of the ankle giving way (chronic instability).

In the first few days

  • Support the ankle with tape or an ankle brace, and put weight on it as far as the pain allows.
  • Use crutches for the first few days if you need to, but gradually put weight on the foot.
  • Intermittent ice and raising the leg may help reduce swelling.
  • Keep the ankle moving from early on with ankle pumps.
  • If you need pain relief, ask your doctor.

Grades of sprain

Sprains are divided into three grades according to the damage to the ligament. The grade gives an idea of recovery time and how much support is needed. According to the guidelines, ligament damage is assessed most accurately by an examination 4–5 days after the injury.

I

Mild

Partial, mild damage to the ligament; mild tenderness and swelling. You can put weight on it and walk with little pain; no looseness in the joint.

II

Moderate

A more marked but incomplete tear of the ligament; moderate pain and swelling, mild to moderate bruising. Putting weight on it and walking are painful; there may be mild to moderate looseness.

III

Severe

A complete tear of the ligament; marked swelling and bruising, unable to put weight on it or walk, clear looseness of the joint on examination.

Do you need an X-ray?

Most sprains involve no fracture and do not need an X-ray. The Ottawa ankle rules were developed to identify the patients who do need an X-ray, and in a review combining 27 studies they picked up 97.6% of fractures. Using only part of the rule (for example, only whether you can walk) lowers its accuracy; the whole rule should be applied.

Ankle X-ray: if there is pain in the ankle area together with bony tenderness along the back edge of the lower 6 centimetres of the shin bone or the outer leg bone, or at the tip of the inner or outer ankle bone (malleolus), or if 4 steps cannot be taken (even with a limp) both right after the injury and at the examination. Foot X-ray: if there is pain in the middle of the foot together with tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal (the bump on the outer edge of the foot) or the navicular bone (the bump on the inner edge of the foot), or if 4 steps cannot be taken in the same way.

How long does it take?

According to data reported in the US physiotherapy guideline, only about half of people who sprain an ankle seek medical care. Return to work or sport can vary from 1 day to a little over 3 weeks depending on the activity. However, the pain going away does not mean the ankle has fully recovered: pain lasts 1 year or longer in 5–33% of people, and full recovery at about 3 years is reported in 50–85%. With severe sprains, recovery can take months.

Chronic instability: In a study that followed people after their first sprain, 40% had developed chronic instability at 12 months, with the ankle repeatedly giving way and spraining, while 60% recovered without problems. Balance and neuromuscular control exercises matter both for recovery and for preventing this.

Past to present

A short history of sprain treatment

For a long time, rest and immobilisation were at the forefront of sprain care. Research over more than the last twenty years has clarified how to decide on an X-ray and shown the importance of early, gradual movement and balance exercises.

  1. 2003In a review combining 27 studies and 15,581 people, the Ottawa ankle rules picked up 97.6% of fractures; it was concluded that the rules are reliable for identifying patients who do not need an X-ray.
  2. 2009In a study of 584 patients with severe sprains at 8 emergency departments in the United Kingdom, a below-knee cast for 10 days gave faster recovery than a tubular elastic bandage, and an Aircast-type ankle brace gave better function at 3 months. By 9 months there was no difference between the groups. The authors recommended the below-knee cast, which showed the broadest benefit.
  3. 2010In a study of 101 people with mild to moderate sprains, those given an early exercise programme in the first week had better function at weeks 1 and 2 than those given conventional care of protection, rest, ice, compression and elevation.
  4. 2017A study combining 46 systematic reviews found strong evidence for ankle braces and moderate evidence for neuromuscular control (balance) exercises in preventing repeat sprains.
  5. 2018An evidence-based guideline was updated (BJSM): ligament damage is best assessed by an examination 4–5 days after the injury; supervised exercise programmes are preferred over passive treatments; surgery is reserved for those who do not respond to thorough exercise-based treatment.
  6. 2021The guideline of the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) was updated (JOSPT): external support with gradual loading, structured exercise and manual therapy were recommended at the strongest level (A); not using ultrasound therapy was recommended (A).

Treatment

The 2021 guideline (JOSPT) of the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) groups treatment after a sprain under the following headings.

  • Protection and gradual weight-bearingProtecting the ankle with external support such as tape or an ankle brace and gradually putting weight on the foot are recommended (A). Severe injuries may need immobilisation for up to 10 days.
  • ExerciseA structured programme including protected active movement, neuromuscular control and balance exercises is recommended (A).
  • Manual therapyLymphatic drainage, soft tissue and joint mobilisations are applied together with exercise, within a pain-free range (A).
  • Ice and medicationIntermittent ice can be used together with exercise (C). Ask your physician whether anti-inflammatory painkillers are suitable for you (C).
  • Returning to work and sportWork- and sport-specific training is planned from the early stage of rehabilitation; an ankle brace can be used on return (B).
  • Preventing further sprainsUsing an ankle brace and doing a balance-focused exercise programme are recommended for people who have had a sprain (A).

Treatment options: what do the guidelines say?

The recommendations of the guideline of the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) (JOSPT, 2021) and of an evidence-based guideline update published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (2018), side by side. The letters in brackets are the recommendation grades in the JOSPT guideline (A strong, B moderate, C weak, D conflicting evidence). The second guideline's recommendations were taken from its abstract; treatment is chosen for each person.

Diagnosis and X-rays

JOSPT
The whole Ottawa rule should be used to decide on an X-ray; the examination is most accurate 4–5 days after the injury.
BJSM guideline
Ligament damage is best assessed by an examination after 4–5 days.

Support and immobilisation

JOSPT
Tape or an ankle brace and gradual loading (A); severe injuries may need immobilisation for up to 10 days.
BJSM guideline
A short period of immobilisation may help reduce pain and swelling; with a ligament tear, tape or an ankle brace together with an exercise programme is most beneficial.

Exercise

JOSPT
A structured programme of protected active movement, neuromuscular control and balance (A).
BJSM guideline
Supervised exercise programmes should be preferred over passive treatments.

Manual therapy

JOSPT
Lymphatic drainage, soft tissue and joint mobilisation within a pain-free range (A); in long-lasting instability, joint mobilisation for short-term movement and balance (A).

Ice and medication

JOSPT
Intermittent, repeated ice together with exercise (C); anti-inflammatory painkillers may be given by an authorised professional (C).
BJSM guideline
Anti-inflammatory painkillers can reduce pain and swelling, but they carry a risk of side effects and may suppress natural healing.
Note
Ask your physician which is suitable for you.

Devices and acupuncture

JOSPT
Ultrasound therapy should not be used (A); low-level laser for pain in the early phase (C); shortwave diathermy for swelling (C); conflicting evidence for electrotherapy and acupuncture (D).

Surgery

BJSM guideline
Should be reserved for cases that do not respond to thorough, exercise-based treatment.

Preventing another sprain

JOSPT
For people who have had a sprain, an ankle brace together with balance and neuromuscular control exercises (A); for people who have never sprained but are at risk, an ankle brace (A) and balance exercises (C).
BJSM guideline
An ankle brace should be considered an effective option for preventing repeated sprains.

Exercises at home

Six exercises for your ankle

You can start the first two exercises early on. As pain and swelling ease, move on to the strengthening and balance exercises. For exercises done standing, hold on to something sturdy; stop if you feel sharp pain.

Ankle pumps and circles

Sit or lie down with your leg straight. Pull your toes towards you and push them away. In the first days, do only this up-and-down movement within a pain-free range; as the pain eases, you can also draw small circles with your ankle without forcing it. It helps reduce swelling and maintain movement.

10 reps in each direction, 3–4 times a day

Towel calf stretch

Sit with your legs straight, loop a towel around the front of your foot and hold the ends. Keeping your knee straight, pull the towel towards you; feel a stretch in your calf. Stay within a pain-free range.

30 seconds, 3 reps

Resistance band eversion

Sit down, loop a resistance band around the front of your foot and secure the other end on the inner side (under your other foot or to a fixed piece of furniture). Keeping your heel on the floor and without moving your knee, turn your foot outwards from the ankle and slowly bring it back. It strengthens the muscles on the outer side of the ankle.

10–15 reps, 2–3 sets

Heel raises

Holding on to the counter, rise up onto your toes on both feet, then lower slowly. As it gets easier, do it on your sprained foot only.

10–15 reps, 2–3 sets

Standing on one leg

Stand close to a counter and balance on your sprained foot. As it gets easier, increase the difficulty one step at a time: first let go of the support, then close your eyes or try on a soft surface such as a folded towel; keep your hand close to the counter while trying these. People with balance problems and older adults should not do these stages without support. It is the key exercise for preventing new sprains.

30 seconds, 3–5 reps

Heel-to-toe walking

Along the counter or a wall, walk in a straight line, placing the heel of your front foot just in front of the toes of your back foot.

10–15 steps, 2–3 rounds

Returning to sport and daily life

The pain going away does not mean the ankle has fully recovered. Returning gradually reduces the risk of new sprains.

  • Walk on flat ground first, then move on to light jogging, and finally to changes of direction and jumping.
  • Do not start running until you can balance comfortably on your sprained foot.
  • Use an ankle brace or tape for support for a while when returning to sport.
  • Even after the pain has gone, keep doing balance exercises a few times a week.
  • Take care on uneven ground and in the dark; wear shoes that support the heel.

Frequently asked questions

Should I put weight on my sprained foot?

If a fracture is not suspected, yes, as far as the pain allows. If you cannot take 4 steps after the injury or there is clear tenderness over the bone, you first need to be assessed for a fracture (see "Do I need an X-ray?"). The American Physical Therapy Association guideline recommends protecting a newly sprained ankle with support such as tape or an ankle brace and gradually putting weight on the foot. Not putting any weight on it for a long time can delay recovery.

Do I need to have an X-ray?

Most sprains do not need one. According to the Ottawa rules, an ankle X-ray is needed if there is pain in the ankle area together with bony tenderness along the back edge of the lower 6 centimetres of the shin bone or the outer leg bone, or at the tip of the inner or outer ankle bone (malleolus), or if 4 steps cannot be taken (even with a limp) both right after the injury and at the examination. A foot X-ray is needed if there is pain in the middle of the foot together with tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal (the bump on the outer edge of the foot) or the navicular bone (the bump on the inner edge of the foot). These rules pick up almost all fractures.

How long does it take to heal?

It depends on the grade of the sprain. According to the US physiotherapy guideline, return to work or sport can range from 1 day to a little over 3 weeks depending on the activity. However, recovery may not be complete even when the pain has gone: in studies, pain lasted 1 year or longer in 5–33% of people, and full recovery at about 3 years was reported in 50–85%. With severe sprains, recovery can take months. This is why it is important to keep up balance and strengthening exercises after the pain has gone.

If a ligament is torn, do I need surgery?

Usually not. Even complete ligament tears are generally treated without surgery: short-term support, gradual loading and an exercise programme. The guideline published in BJSM in 2018 reserves surgery for cases that do not respond to thorough, properly delivered exercise-based treatment.

Should I use ice?

Intermittent, repeated ice together with an exercise programme is among the methods the guideline lists as usable to reduce pain and swelling. Do not put ice directly on the skin; keep a thin cloth in between. England's National Health Service (NHS) recommends applying it for up to 20 minutes every 2–3 hours.

Should I use an ankle brace?

Using support such as tape or an ankle brace for a new sprain is recommended. For people who have had a sprain, wearing an ankle brace during sport and doing balance exercises are also recommended to prevent new sprains. A study combining 46 systematic reviews found strong evidence for ankle braces and moderate evidence for neuromuscular control (balance) exercises in preventing repeat sprains; for a new sprain, the evidence for early movement was strong.

Why do I keep spraining the same ankle?

If balance, muscle strength and the ankle's sense of position have not fully recovered after a sprain, the ankle becomes prone to spraining again. Keeping up balance and strengthening exercises even after the pain has gone and returning to sport gradually help break this cycle. In footballers, the FIFA 11+ warm-up programme done regularly before training reduced the overall injury rate by 39% in a meta-analysis combining 4 studies; these studies were not specific to people who had had a previous sprain.

When to seek help right away

Go to the emergency department straight away:

  • Visible deformity of the ankle
  • Numbness, tingling, coldness or colour change in the foot (circulation or a nerve may be affected)
  • If you heard a snap or crack at the moment of injury
  • Increasing pain, numbness or bluish colour inside a cast, splint or bandage (loosen the bandage; if it does not improve, seek help immediately)
  • If you cannot take 4 steps after the injury (even with a limp) or there is clear tenderness over the bone (it may be a fracture; an X-ray is needed)
  • Pain at the back of the heel that started with a "pop", and being unable to rise onto your toes (possible Achilles tendon rupture)
  • Swelling, pain and redness in the calf (this may be a blood clot)
  • Fever and spreading redness
  • Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain (it may be a blood clot in the lung; call 112 immediately)

See a physician soon:

  • If pain or swelling is getting worse or does not start to ease within a few days
  • If there is no clear improvement within about 2 weeks
  • If you have diabetes or loss of feeling in the foot
  • Frequent giving way of the ankle or repeated sprains (there may be accompanying ligament or cartilage damage, or chronic instability)
