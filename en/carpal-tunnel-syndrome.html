Condition guide

Carpal tunnel syndrome

It occurs when the median nerve is compressed in a narrow channel in the wrist (the carpal tunnel). It causes numbness, tingling and pain in the thumb, index and middle fingers; symptoms are typically worse at night. In mild to moderate cases, a night splint and adjusting daily load are the first step; in cases that do not settle or that progress, the nerve can be relieved with a minor operation.

What is it?

On the palm side of the wrist, there is a narrow channel formed by bones and a thick ligament. The tendons that bend the fingers and the median nerve pass through this channel. When the pressure inside the channel rises, the nerve is squeezed and symptoms start in the fingers that the nerve supplies.

If the nerve stays markedly compressed for a long time, the numbness can become permanent and the muscles at the base of the thumb can weaken; this is why worsening symptoms should be assessed.

Symptoms and risk factors

  • Numbness and tingling in the thumb, index finger, middle finger and half of the ring finger
  • Symptoms that get worse at night, wake you from sleep and ease when you shake your hand
  • Numbness that gets worse when you hold a phone, drive or read a book
  • Weakness in the thumb, difficulty gripping, dropping things
  • Risk factors: excess weight, pregnancy, jobs that involve repetitive gripping, joint inflammation (arthritis), diabetes, a family history, a previous wrist injury

How is it recognised?

The diagnosis is mostly based on where and when the symptoms occur and on an examination. Marking on a hand diagram which parts of the hand are affected also helps the assessment.

CTS-6 score

Six findings are assessed together: numbness mainly in the fingers supplied by the median nerve, numbness at night, wasting or weakness of the muscles at the base of the thumb, a positive Phalen test, reduced ability to tell two points apart, and a positive Tinel sign. The 2024 AAOS guideline states that this score can be used instead of routine EMG or ultrasound. It is a tool used during an examination, not for self-diagnosis.

Examination tests

In the Phalen test the wrists are held bent for 30–60 seconds; in the Tinel test the palm side of the wrist is tapped lightly. The test is positive if numbness or tingling appears in the fingers. No test is accurate enough on its own; the findings are interpreted together.

When are EMG and ultrasound needed?

Nerve conduction studies and EMG, or ultrasound, are not needed for every patient. They are requested when the diagnosis is uncertain or when the result would change treatment (for example, the decision about surgery). According to AAOS, MRI should not be used for diagnosis.

Does it go away on its own?

In some people it can improve on its own. In a study that followed 196 untreated patients (274 hands) for 10–15 months, symptoms and nerve measurements generally improved on their own; however, measurements worsened in those whose findings were mild at the start. A short duration of symptoms and younger age were linked with a better course; symptoms in both hands and a positive Phalen test were linked with a worse course.

In pregnancy: Symptoms usually ease after giving birth as the swelling goes down. However, in a study that followed 45 women, about half still had symptoms 3 years after giving birth (only one had needed surgery). This is why symptoms that do not go away after birth should also be assessed.

Does computer use lead to carpal tunnel syndrome?

The expert group that prepared the 2024 guideline of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), noting the lack of reliable research, holds the view that heavy keyboard use is not linked with carpal tunnel syndrome; this is the lowest level of evidence in the guideline. In the 2016 AAOS guideline, by contrast, computer work was listed among the risk factors with moderate evidence. So the question is not settled.

Even so, keeping the wrist bent for long periods can make symptoms worse. Keeping your wrist straight and taking frequent breaks when using a keyboard and mouse may help ease existing symptoms. The 2026 physiotherapy guideline gives a weak recommendation for options such as keyboards that need less force, touch screens, or switching the mouse between hands in people with symptoms.

Past to present

A short history of carpal tunnel syndrome

Nerve compression at the wrist has been known for about 170 years, but the condition only became widely recognised in the middle of the 20th century. In recent years, research has shown more clearly the place of splints, injections and surgery.

  1. 1854James Paget described two cases of median nerve compression at the wrist: in one, a cord tied tightly around the wrist, and in the other, excess bone formed after a wrist fracture, was pressing on the nerve.
  2. 1913Pierre Marie and Charles Foix described wasting of the muscles at the base of the thumb caused by involvement of the median nerve at the level of the ligament in the wrist, and suggested that early division of this ligament might prevent progression.
  3. 1924The first carpal tunnel release operation is reported to have been performed by Herbert Galloway.
  4. 1947Brain, Wright and Wilkinson showed that the median nerve can be compressed at the wrist even without an obvious external cause, leading to sensory disturbance and wasting of the thumb muscles; they reported six cases treated with surgery.
  5. 1950–1966George Phalen clarified the diagnosis and treatment of the condition through large series of patients and brought it wide recognition. The Phalen test, done by holding the wrists bent, bears his name. The name "carpal tunnel syndrome" was first used in a 1953 publication by Kremer.
  6. 2002In a study of 176 patients in the Netherlands, open surgery gave better results than a night splint: success at 18 months was 90% versus 75%. By then, 41% of the splint group had also had surgery.
  7. 2009In a study of 116 patients, surgery gave better results for hand function and symptoms at 12 months than non-surgical treatment including hand therapy and ultrasound therapy; the difference was modest, and those treated without surgery also improved.
  8. 2018In the INSTINCTS trial of 234 people, a single cortisone injection gave more relief than a night splint at 6 weeks; by 6 months there was no difference between the groups.
  9. 2024The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) updated its guideline: the examination-based CTS-6 score can be used instead of routine EMG and ultrasound; cortisone and PRP injections have no long-term benefit; mini-open surgery and closed (endoscopic) surgery give the same results.
  10. 2026The physiotherapy guideline of the orthopaedic and hand and upper extremity academies of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) was updated: night splints are recommended for mild to moderate cases; manual therapy, laser, shockwave and taping receive weak recommendations for short- or medium-term benefit; magnet therapy is recommended against.

Treatment

In mild and moderate cases, treatment starts with non-surgical methods. If there is persistent numbness or muscle wasting, a surgical assessment takes priority.

  • Night splintA night splint that keeps the wrist straight (neutral) is recommended in the 2026 physiotherapy guideline to improve symptoms and hand function in the short and medium term in mild to moderate cases (grade B); it can also be used by people waiting for surgery. According to England's National Health Service (NHS), a splint may take up to 6 weeks to help.
  • Adjusting your daily loadReducing tasks that require repeatedly bending the wrist and gripping hard may relieve the nerve.
  • Manual therapyDepending on the examination findings, manual therapy aimed at places along the neck and arm where the nerve may be compressed can be used for short-term benefit in mild to moderate cases; the recommendation in the guideline is weak (C), and according to AAOS no long-term benefit has been shown.
  • ExerciseTendon and nerve gliding exercises are widely used, but the evidence for their effectiveness is limited and of very low quality. They should be done as support, without making symptoms worse.
  • Other physical therapy methodsIn the 2026 physiotherapy guideline, laser, interferential current and superficial heat receive weak recommendations (C) for short-term benefit, and shockwave and, in mild cases, taping for short- and medium-term benefit; taping does not replace a splint. According to AAOS, no long-term benefit of these methods has been shown. Magnet therapy is not recommended.
  • Cortisone injectionIt can reduce symptoms quickly: in one study a single injection gave more relief than a night splint at 6 weeks, but there was no difference at 6 months. According to the 2024 AAOS guideline, it has no long-term benefit.
  • SurgeryThe nerve is relieved by releasing the ligament on the palm side of the wrist. In suitable patients it gives more benefit than non-surgical treatment at 6 and 12 months (AAOS, 2016). No difference was found in patient-reported outcomes between mini-open surgery and the closed (endoscopic) method; the operation can also be done under local anaesthetic alone. AAOS recommends against using a splint or sling after surgery and against routinely giving supervised therapy.
  • During pregnancyIn pregnancy-related carpal tunnel syndrome, symptoms usually ease after birth, but in some women they can last a long time. A night splint is a good option during this period; the physiotherapy guideline recommends follow-up after birth as well.

Treatment options: what do the guidelines say?

The recommendations of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons guideline (AAOS, 2024) and the physiotherapy guideline prepared by the academies of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) (JOSPT, 2026), side by side. The letters in brackets are the recommendation grades in the physiotherapy guideline (A strong, B moderate, C weak); details of this guideline were taken from a secondary summary. For most methods AAOS looks at long-term results, while the physiotherapy guideline looks at short-term benefit; treatment is chosen for each person.

Diagnosis

AAOS
The CTS-6 score can be used instead of routine ultrasound or EMG (strong); MRI and nerve tension tests should not be used for diagnosis (moderate).
Physiotherapy (JOSPT)
CTS-6 (B); the Phalen, Tinel and wrist compression tests should be used to help with diagnosis (B), but none is enough on its own; a fine-filament (monofilament) sensory test to measure sensory loss (A). EMG and ultrasound if the result would change treatment.

Splint

Physiotherapy (JOSPT)
In mild to moderate cases, a night splint that keeps the wrist straight (B); if not enough, wearing it during the day too or changing its position (C); a splint in pregnancy and follow-up after birth (C).
AAOS
A splint or sling should not be used after surgery (moderate).

Exercise and manual therapy

Physiotherapy (JOSPT)
Manual therapy to the neck and arm for short-term benefit (C); a stretching programme together with a splint if there is no muscle wasting and two-point discrimination is normal (C); no clear recommendation for tendon and nerve gliding.
AAOS
No long-term benefit of exercise, manual therapy and massage has been shown (limited evidence).

Laser, shockwave and other devices

Physiotherapy (JOSPT)
Laser, interferential current and superficial heat for short-term benefit, shockwave for short- and medium-term benefit (C); corticosteroid iontophoresis and phonophoresis not recommended (A); magnets not recommended (B).
AAOS
No long-term benefit of laser, shockwave and ultrasound therapy has been shown (limited evidence).

Taping and work adjustments

Physiotherapy (JOSPT)
Kinesio tape in mild cases for short- and medium-term relief, not instead of a splint (C); keyboard and mouse options that need less force (C).
AAOS
No link with heavy keyboard use (expert opinion).

Cortisone and PRP injections

AAOS
A cortisone injection does not give long-term improvement (strong); a PRP injection has no long-term benefit (strong); oral cortisone has no long-term benefit (limited).
Note
In the short term, a single cortisone injection can give faster relief than a night splint (INSTINCTS, 2018). The physiotherapy guideline does not cover injections.

Surgery

AAOS
Mini-open and closed (endoscopic) surgery give the same results (strong); local anaesthetic alone may be enough (strong); routine supervised therapy after surgery is not recommended (moderate).
Physiotherapy (JOSPT, summary)
Medical assessment is emphasised for progressive sensory loss, increasing weakness and wasting of the muscles at the base of the thumb; the guideline does not cover rehabilitation after surgery.

Exercises at home

Four exercises for carpal tunnel syndrome

These movements should be gentle. If numbness and tingling increase during or after exercise, make the movement smaller or stop that exercise. Do them as a support to the night splint and to adjusting your daily load.

Tendon gliding

Hold your hand upright with your fingers straight. Move through the following positions in turn, holding each for 5 seconds: keeping the knuckles at the base of the fingers straight, bend the middle and end joints to make a hook; make a full fist; keep your fingers straight and bend them only at the base knuckles to 90 degrees (tabletop position); make a fist with the fingertips staying straight; then open your hand. It helps the finger tendons glide easily through the tunnel. Stop if your symptoms get worse.

10 rounds, 3–5 times a day

Median nerve glide

Stand upright and hold your arm out to the side, a little below shoulder height, with your palm facing up; your elbow can be slightly bent. As you slowly bend your wrist back, tilt your head towards the same side, towards your arm; as you release your wrist, bring your head back upright. This way the nerve is tightened at one end while it is slackened at the other, so it glides without being stretched. If tingling or numbness increases, make the movement smaller or stop.

10 reps, 2–3 times a day

Wrist flexor stretch

Hold your arm out in front of you with the elbow straight and palm facing up. With your other hand, gently pull your fingers down and back; feel a mild stretch on the inner side of your forearm. You can start with your elbow slightly bent; stop if tingling starts.

15–20 seconds, 3 reps

Thumb stretch

Place your hand on a table, palm facing up. With your other hand, gently open your thumb outwards and backwards; feel a mild stretch at the base of the thumb. Do not force it if the joint at the base of your thumb hurts.

15–20 seconds, 3 reps

Looking after your wrist in everyday life

The aim is not to stop using your hand, but to cut down on positions that increase the pressure on the nerve.

  • Try not to sleep with your wrist bent; a splint helps with this.
  • Do not hold your phone in the same hand with your wrist bent for long periods; take frequent breaks.
  • When you use a keyboard and mouse, keep your wrist straight and support your forearms.
  • Break up tasks that need strong, repetitive gripping and, if possible, take turns using each hand.
  • When symptoms start, shaking your hand and changing position may bring relief.

Frequently asked questions

Does carpal tunnel syndrome go away on its own?

In some people it can improve on its own. In a study that followed 196 untreated patients (274 hands) for 10–15 months, symptoms and nerve measurements generally improved on their own; however, measurements worsened in those whose findings were mild at the start. A short duration of symptoms and younger age were linked with a better course, while symptoms in both hands and a positive Phalen test were linked with a worse course. With long-lasting or steadily increasing numbness and weakness, treatment should not be delayed, so that the nerve is not permanently damaged.

How long should I wear a night splint?

A splint that keeps the wrist straight is worn mainly at night. According to England's National Health Service (NHS), a splint may take up to 6 weeks to help. According to the 2026 physiotherapy guideline, if a night splint is not enough, wearing it during the day too or changing the splint's position may be considered; decide this together with your physiotherapist.

Does using a computer cause carpal tunnel syndrome?

The expert group that prepared the 2024 guideline of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), noting the lack of reliable research, holds the view that heavy keyboard use is not linked with carpal tunnel syndrome; this is the lowest level of evidence in the guideline. In the 2016 AAOS guideline, by contrast, computer work was listed among the risk factors. According to the NHS, jobs involving repeated gripping, excess weight and pregnancy are among the risk factors.

Do cortisone injections work?

A cortisone injection can reduce symptoms quickly: in a study of 234 people, a single injection gave more relief than a night splint at 6 weeks; by 6 months there was no difference between the groups. According to the 2024 AAOS guideline, cortisone injections have no long-term benefit; symptoms can return over time. You can make the decision together with your physician.

When is surgery needed?

If there is thinning of the muscles at the base of the thumb, constant numbness, weakness, or symptoms that persist despite a splint and other treatments, an assessment by a hand surgeon is needed. According to the 2016 AAOS guideline, the benefit of surgery at 6 and 12 months is greater than that of non-surgical treatments. In a study of 176 patients in the Netherlands, success at 18 months was 90% in the surgery group and 75% in the splint group; however, 41% of the splint group had had surgery by then.

Do exercises help?

Tendon and nerve gliding exercises are widely used, but according to a Cochrane review the evidence for their effectiveness is limited and of very low quality; the 2026 physiotherapy guideline also makes no clear recommendation for these exercises. The guideline gives a weak recommendation for a stretching programme together with a splint in mild to moderate cases without wasting of the thumb-base muscles and with normal two-point discrimination. Exercises should be done as support for the splint and load adjustment, without increasing pain and numbness.

Does carpal tunnel syndrome in pregnancy go away after birth?

Symptoms usually ease after birth as the swelling goes down; during this period a night splint can be used until the hand returns to normal. However, in a study that followed 45 women, about half still had symptoms 3 years after giving birth; only one had needed surgery. If symptoms do not go away, assessment after birth is also important.

Do I need an EMG?

Not always. According to the 2024 AAOS guideline, the examination-based CTS-6 score can be used instead of routine EMG or ultrasound. A nerve conduction study is requested when the diagnosis is uncertain or when the result would change treatment (for example, the decision about surgery).

When to seek help right away

The following may show that the nerve is seriously affected or that the numbness has another cause.

Go to the emergency department straight away or call 112:

  • Sudden weakness of the arm or face, numbness, difficulty speaking (it may be a stroke; call 112)
  • Rapidly increasing numbness, swelling and severe pain after a wrist injury or fracture
  • Redness, warmth and swelling in the wrist together with a fever

See a physician soon:

  • Constant numbness, or thinning or wasting of the muscles at the base of the thumb
  • Marked loss of strength in the hand, often dropping things, difficulty doing up buttons
  • Numbness spreading into the arm together with neck pain (it may come from the neck)
  • Numbness in both hands and the legs, clumsy hands or unsteadiness when walking (it may come from the spinal cord or a nerve disease; if there are problems with bladder or bowel control, go to the emergency department)
