30-second chair stand test

How many times can you stand up from a chair in 30 seconds? This simple test measures the strength and endurance of your legs. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) uses it to assess fall risk in older adults. Your phone’s camera can count your repetitions for you; the video never leaves your device.

Try it with or without the camera

With the camera: place your phone 2–3 metres away at waist height so that it sees you from the side, from head to feet. Without the camera: someone next to you taps the screen each time you stand; if you are alone, count your stands yourself and enter the result when the time is up. The camera count can miss a stand or count an extra one; check the number when the test ends and correct it with − and + if needed.

    How to do it

    1. Put a sturdy chair with no armrests and a straight back against a wall so it cannot slide (seat height about 43 cm).
    2. Sit in the middle of the chair with your feet flat on the floor. Cross your arms over your chest, with each hand on the opposite shoulder.
    3. At the signal, stand up fully, then sit back down. Repeat for 30 seconds.
    4. Only full stands count. If you are more than halfway up when the time runs out, that stand also counts. If you need to use your arms to stand up, the test stops and the result is 0.

    Doing it safely

    • The CDC recommends that someone stands next to you during the test. If you have fallen recently, feel dizzy or have balance problems, do not do the test alone.
    • Stop at once if you have chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness or marked knee or hip pain.
    • For the camera, avoid having a bright window behind you; loose clothing can hide your hips and knees.
    • Don’t race: stand up and sit down quickly but with control, and don’t drop onto the chair.

    Strengthen your legs

    Four exercises at home

    Do them 2–3 days a week, within a pain-free range. After a few weeks, you can repeat the test to see your progress. For balance, see the 10-second balance test; for your fall risk, see the fall prevention guide.

    Sit-to-stand from a chair

    The test itself is its best exercise. On a sturdy chair without armrests, with your arms crossed over your chest, stand up and sit down at a controlled pace. If this is too hard, start by pushing up with your arms or by using a higher chair.

    8–12 repetitions, 2–3 sets, 2–3 days a week

    Wall sit

    Lean your back against a wall, step your feet slightly forward and slide down, bending your knees as far as is comfortable. Keep your knees above your ankles and don’t hold your breath.

    20–30 seconds, 3–4 times

    Bridge

    Lie on your back with your knees bent and your feet on the floor. Squeeze your buttocks and lift your hips off the floor so that your shoulders, hips and knees form a straight line; lower slowly.

    10–12 repetitions, 2–3 sets

    Heel raises

    Stand tall, holding lightly onto a worktop. Rise up onto your toes, then lower slowly. When it gets easy, try it on one foot.

    10–15 reps, 2–3 sets

    Frequently asked questions

    Is my camera image sent anywhere?

    No. The image is processed only on your own device, inside your browser; it is not recorded or sent to any server. The first time you turn on the camera, the motion detection software (Google’s open-source MediaPipe model, about 17 MB in total) is downloaded to your device; all the counting happens on your device. Your results are also stored only on this device.

    What if the camera counts wrongly?

    The camera counter is a helper, not a perfect one. Loose clothing, poor light or your legs moving out of view can upset the count. When the test ends, you can correct the number yourself. The most reliable way is for someone next to you to count as well.

    What if I can’t stand up without using my arms?

    Under the CDC’s STEADI protocol, if you need to use your arms to stand up, the test is stopped and the result is recorded as 0. This shows that your leg strength needs support; it would be a good idea to have an assessment with a physiotherapist or doctor.

    Can this test tell me for certain whether I will fall?

    No. The UK national health guideline from NICE (2025) advises against using a test or scoring tool on its own to predict falls risk; tests like this help to spot problems with walking and balance. So if you have fallen in the past year, tell your doctor whatever your result. If you have fallen more than once, were injured in a fall, lost consciousness or could not get up from the floor without help, you can ask for a full falls assessment.

    Can I improve my result?

    Yes. Leg strength can improve with exercise at any age. You can start with the exercises below and repeat the test every few weeks to follow your progress. In a study of people with hip osteoarthritis, an increase of 2 stands was considered a meaningful improvement.
