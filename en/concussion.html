Concussion: recovery and a gradual return to activity

Do you have a headache, dizziness, a dazed feeling or trouble concentrating after a blow to the head, a fall or a sudden jolt? Concussion is an injury in which the brain is temporarily affected. Symptoms usually clear within a few days to a few weeks. The long, strict rest that used to be advised is no longer recommended: after 24–48 hours of relative rest, returning step by step to everyday tasks and light activity helps recovery.

Check these first

If any of the following apply after a blow to the head, get medical help straight away rather than using this guide. Do not drive yourself to the emergency department.

Call 112

  • The person was knocked out and has not woken up, or is struggling to stay awake
  • They have had a seizure (fit)
  • They have difficulty speaking, understanding, walking or keeping their balance, or numbness or weakness in part of the body
  • They have problems with their vision or hearing
  • Clear fluid or blood is coming from the ears or nose, or there is bruising behind the ears
  • They fell from more than 1 metre or 5 stairs, or were hit at speed, such as in a car or bike accident

Go to the emergency department

  • The person was knocked out briefly but has woken up
  • They have vomited, or keep vomiting
  • They have a headache that painkillers do not ease, or that keeps getting worse
  • They have memory problems, increasing drowsiness, irritability or a change in behaviour; a baby or young child is crying more than usual
  • They take blood-thinning medicine, have a bleeding disorder or have had brain surgery
  • They had alcohol or drugs just before the injury

Symptoms

  • Headache, dizziness, nausea.
  • Feeling dazed or “foggy”, difficulty concentrating and remembering.
  • Sensitivity to light or noise, tiredness, changes in sleep.
  • Changes in mood, such as irritability, anxiety or tearfulness.

Symptoms usually start within 24 hours, but can be delayed by up to 3 weeks. Mild symptoms can last up to 2 weeks; most people recover within 4 weeks.

The first 24–48 hours

  • Have an adult with you for the first 24 hours; do not stay on your own.
  • Rest and sleep as much as you need; keep phone, computer and screen time to a minimum.
  • Do not drink alcohol; do not take sleeping pills unless your doctor recommends them.
  • Do not ride a bicycle or operate machinery for the first 24 hours; do not drive until you have fully recovered.
  • For a headache you can take paracetamol or ibuprofen at the dose on the packet; you can hold ice wrapped in a towel to any swelling.

A gradual return to activity and sport

The UK guidelines for grassroots sport, produced with government support, describe the return in six stages. It is fine for your symptoms to rise slightly as you move on to the next stage, as long as the activity makes them no more than mildly worse and the increase settles within minutes to a few hours. If symptoms get clearly worse, go back to the previous stage.

  • 1. Relative restFirst 24–48 hours: calm activities, minimal screen time.
  • 2. Everyday tasks and light activityShort, easy reading, short walks, light household tasks.
  • 3. Building toleranceMental tasks in blocks of 20–30 minutes; 10–15 minutes of light aerobic exercise (walking, exercise bike).
  • 4. Part-time school or work, non-contact trainingOnce you have no symptoms at rest, usually around week 2.
  • 5. Full-time school or work, unrestricted trainingDay 15 at the earliest; after 14 days without symptoms at rest.
  • 6. Return to competitionNot before day 21; after 14 days without symptoms at rest.

Returning to school or work comes before returning to sport. The guidelines’ principle is clear: if in doubt, sit them out. Someone with suspected concussion does not go back to the game, training or PE that day, and stays away from them for at least 24 hours.

What does the research show?

According to the international consensus agreed in Amsterdam in 2022 and published in 2023, strict rest is not recommended; everyday tasks and light aerobic exercise such as walking or an exercise bike may help recovery, and limiting screen time in the first 48 hours may also help. The consensus recommends neck and balance rehabilitation for people whose dizziness, neck pain or headache lasts longer than 10 days, and an assessment involving different specialties for those whose symptoms last more than 4 weeks.

One of the studies behind these recommendations involved 103 young athletes in the USA: those who did about 20 minutes of aerobic exercise a day within the first 10 days, at an intensity that did not worsen their symptoms (80% of the heart rate at which symptoms increased on a treadmill test), recovered in an average of 13 days, compared with 17 days for those who only stretched. Recovery took longer than 4 weeks for 2 of 52 people in the exercise group and 7 of 51 in the stretching group, but this difference was not statistically significant. The studies were mostly done in young athletes; evidence in adults and in injuries outside sport is more limited.

Exercises at home

Six exercises for light movement and balance

These movements are started in stages 2 and 3, after the rest period. The head movement and balance exercises come from a children’s hospital information sheet on balance after concussion. Each movement should make your symptoms no more than mildly worse; if dizziness persists or you are afraid of falling, get checked by a physiotherapist or doctor.

Short walk

Walk at a comfortable pace on a flat route. If your symptoms rise no more than slightly and settle quickly, you can make the walk a little longer each day.

Start with a few minutes; 10–15 minutes in stage 3

Gaze stabilisation: head turns

Hold a pen or a card at arm’s length and keep your eyes on it. With your eyes locked on the object, slowly turn your head from side to side. Only speed up when you are ready.

5–10 reps, as tolerated

Looking up and down

Sit upright. Slowly lift your head to look at the ceiling, then lower it to look at the floor. The aim is to help your brain get used to head movement again; any dizziness should stay mild and pass quickly.

5–10 reps

Tilting the head sideways

Sit upright. Slowly bring your ear towards your shoulder, come back to the middle and tilt to the other side. Do not lift your shoulder.

5–10 reps each side

Tandem stance and walk

Stand next to a wall. First stand for 10 seconds with one foot slightly in front of the other, then swap feet. When this gets easier, walk along a corridor heel to toe, touching the heel to the toes of the front foot.

10 seconds with each foot in front; then 20 steps

Standing on one leg

Holding a sturdy chair, stand on one leg. When it gets easier, let go and hold for longer; later you can make it harder, for example by closing your eyes or drawing letters in the air.

Start with 10 seconds, up to 30 seconds

Frequently asked questions

Don’t I need to rest in a dark room?

No. Long, strict rest is no longer recommended. In a study of 88 patients aged 11–22, those told to rest completely at home for 5 days reported more symptoms, and their symptoms cleared more slowly, than those who rested for 1–2 days and then gradually returned to activity. Rest relatively for the first 24–48 hours and sleep as much as you can; then gradually return to everyday tasks and gentle walks.

Can I use my phone and computer?

Keeping screen time to a minimum in the first 48 hours may help recovery. After that you can use screens for short periods with breaks; in stage 3, mental tasks are recommended in blocks of 20–30 minutes.

When can I go back to school or work?

When you feel better, starting with short periods. The UK grassroots sport guidelines put the return to school and work before the return to sport: once you have no symptoms at rest, usually around week 2, you can go back to school or work part-time. During this time you can ask for adjustments such as extra time for homework and exams.

When can I return to sport?

You return to training gradually; you do not return to contact or competitive sport before day 21 after the injury, and you need to have been free of symptoms at rest for at least 14 days. The NHS also advises staying away from contact sports for at least 3 weeks. Children should avoid rough play for the first few days. According to the international consensus, most people are fully back in their sport within about a month.

Does exercise speed up recovery?

Light aerobic exercise may help. In a study of 103 young people aged 13–18 with sport-related concussion, those who started within the first 10 days and walked or used an exercise bike for about 20 minutes a day, at an intensity that did not worsen their symptoms, recovered in an average of 13 days; for those who only stretched, it took 17 days. The intensity should stay at a level that makes your symptoms no more than mildly worse.

What should I do if my symptoms don’t go away?

See your doctor if symptoms have not improved within 2 weeks or are getting worse; if they last more than 4 weeks, an assessment involving different specialties is recommended. If dizziness, neck pain or headache lasts longer than 10 days, the international consensus recommends neck and balance (cervicovestibular) rehabilitation. In a small study of 31 people aged 12–30 with sport-related concussion, 73% of those who had this rehabilitation were cleared to return to sport within 8 weeks, compared with 7% of those who had usual care only.

For neck pain after a blow to the head, see the whiplash guide; for headache, the headache guide; for brief spinning that starts when you turn your head, the dizziness (BPPV) guide.

When should you see a doctor?

  • Your symptoms are getting worse or have not improved within 2 weeks
  • You are unsure whether it is safe to drive or go back to work, school or sport
  • Your symptoms last longer than 4 weeks: do not return to sport until you have been assessed
  • If any of the emergency signs above appear later, call 112 or go to the emergency department straight away
