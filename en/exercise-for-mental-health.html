Exercise for mental health

Exercise is good not only for the body but also for the mind. Analyses pooling hundreds of studies show that it reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety, and depression is less common among people who are regularly active. This guide explains which exercise helps, how much, and how to do it. Exercise does not replace treatment; it complements it.

Who does it help?

In a review covering 97 systematic reviews and 1,039 studies, physical activity reduced depression, anxiety and psychological distress in many groups. The clearest benefits were seen in people with depression, pregnant and postpartum women, healthy adults, and people with HIV or kidney disease.

In the depression analysis, the effect was similar in people with and without other illnesses, and in those with mild and severe symptoms.

How should we read this?

  • The effects were similar in size to those of cognitive behavioural therapy.
  • Exercise was also effective when combined with medication or therapy.
  • Most studies were small, and participants knew which group they were in, so confidence in the evidence was rated low or very low.
  • Even so, the authors suggest exercise could be considered one of the core treatment options, alongside therapy and medication.

  • Dance showed the largest effect but rests on only 5 studies; promising, not yet certain.
  • Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training were the three types the authors highlighted.
  • Intensity matters: effects were larger with vigorous exercise such as running or interval training, but light exercise such as walking also brought clear benefits.
  • Yoga and strength training were better tolerated; fewer people dropped out of these groups.
  • Strength training appeared more effective in women and younger people, and yoga in men and older people; these subgroup results are not conclusive.

Anxiety

Twelve weeks of exercise for anxiety

A primary care trial in Sweden enrolled 286 people with anxiety disorders; half had lived with anxiety for at least ten years. For 12 weeks, participants joined 60-minute group exercise sessions three times a week, guided by a physiotherapist. Each session combined a warm-up, a 45-minute circuit of cardio and strength stations, and finished with stretching.

  • In both the moderate and the high-intensity groups, anxiety symptoms fell markedly compared with the group that received only advice on physical activity, including in people with long-standing anxiety.
  • The chance of improvement rose 3.6-fold at moderate intensity and 4.9-fold at high intensity.
  • The harder the exercise, the greater the improvement.
  • In the review covering 97 systematic reviews, physical activity also reduced anxiety symptoms to a similar degree as depression.

How much?

The dose: start, even if it is small

1

Something beats nothing

Even people doing half the recommended amount had an 18% lower risk of depression. According to the World Health Organization, any amount of activity is better than none.

2

Aim for 150 minutes

For adults, the target is at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week. That is 30 minutes of brisk walking on five days of the week; build up to it gradually.

3

Push a little

Effects were larger with moderate and vigorous exercise. Start at a comfortable pace and speed up a little over time.

4

Keep going for weeks

For less severe depression, the UK guideline lists group exercise more than once a week for 10 weeks, with a trained practitioner, among the treatment options. The anxiety programme lasted 12 weeks. After that, make movement a habit.

When getting started feels hard

Tiredness and losing interest in things you used to enjoy are common in depression, so starting can feel hard. This is not a matter of willpower. Small, concrete and enjoyable steps make it easier.

  • Choose something you enjoy. Any exercise you can do regularly is worthwhile.
  • Start small. Even a brisk 10-minute walk can help clear your mind.
  • Decide the day and time in advance. For example, “Tuesday and Thursday, before dinner”.
  • Do it with someone. A walk with a friend or a group class builds both routine and connection; see also the social connection page.
  • Get outside. For walks in a park or by the sea, see the nature prescription page.
  • Be kind to yourself. Don’t be hard on yourself about the days you miss; the DOST break is there for difficult days.

Exercises at home

Six movements for your mood

Chosen from the types that showed the most benefit in research: walking and moving to music, strength training and yoga. Try the strength movements on at least two days a week and walking on the other days. Skip any movement that makes your pain worse.

Brisk walking

Wearing comfortable shoes, walk at a pace at which you can talk but would struggle to sing. Let your arms swing freely. If you can, walk outdoors, along a tree-lined path or in a park.

Start with 10 minutes; build up to 20–30 minutes a day

Side steps to music

Put on an upbeat song you like. Step to the side with one foot and bring the other alongside it, then go the other way. Add your arms too. There is no right or wrong; what matters is keeping the rhythm.

For 1–2 songs

Sit-to-stand from a chair

Sit towards the front of a sturdy chair. Lean forward slightly and stand up without using your arms, then sit down slowly. Increase the number of repetitions as it gets easier.

10 reps, 2–3 sets

Wall press-up

Stand an arm’s length from a wall and place your hands on it at chest height. Keep your body straight; bend your elbows to bring yourself towards the wall, then push away. When it gets easy, make it harder by placing your hands on a kitchen worktop.

10 reps, 2–3 sets

Cat–camel

Get on your hands and knees. As you breathe out, round your back towards the ceiling; as you breathe in, let your lower back sink gently. Move with your breath, without rushing.

8–10 reps

Child’s pose

From your hands and knees, slowly move your hips back towards your heels, with your arms stretched out in front. Rest your forehead on the floor or a cushion. Breathe slowly in and out through your nose. Skip it if your knees hurt.

30–60 seconds, 2–3 times

Frequently asked questions

Can exercise replace medication or therapy?

In less severe depression, exercise is one of the recommended options. In moderate and severe depression, therapy and medication are usually recommended, and exercise can be added to them. In research, exercise was also effective when combined with medication or therapy. Don’t stop your medication on your own; talk to your doctor about any change.

How soon will I notice a difference?

The group programme recommended in the UK guideline lasted 10 weeks, and the anxiety trial’s programme 12 weeks. Judge the result after several weeks of regular exercise. In the meantime, even a single brisk walk can help clear your mind.

I have no energy at all; how do I start?

Start very small: a 10-minute walk, or moving for the length of one song. Light exercise works too. Decide in advance when you will do it and, if you can, do it with someone. Don’t be hard on yourself about the days you can’t manage; pick up where you left off the next day.

Which exercise is best?

Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training stood out in research. But the best exercise is one you enjoy and can do regularly. Fewer people dropped out of yoga and strength training.

When should you seek help?

  • If you have thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department straight away.
  • If you have had weeks of unhappiness, hopelessness, loss of interest in things you used to enjoy, or changes in sleep or appetite, see a doctor or a mental health professional.
  • If anxiety or low mood is getting in the way of your work, relationships or daily tasks, don’t put off getting support.
  • If you have chest pain, breathlessness, dizziness or feel faint during exercise, stop and talk to your doctor.
Important: this page is for information only; it does not diagnose and does not replace treatment. Don’t stop any medication without talking to your doctor.
