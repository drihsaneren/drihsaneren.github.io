Getting up after a fall

The first thing to do after a fall is not to rush. If you are not hurt and feel ready, there is a safe way to get up; if you are hurt or cannot get up, there are things you can do until help arrives. This guide takes you through the steps recommended by the NHS one at a time, with questions and pictures. Reading it once before you ever fall makes it easier to know what to do when you need to.

Step by step

You’ve fallen: what now?

First

Don’t rush. Stay where you are for a few minutes, steady your breathing and check whether anything hurts.

Could you have hurt your head, neck, back or hip, or do you think you won’t be able to get up?

Don’t try to get up

Call for help and keep warm

  1. Call for help: If someone else is at home, shout to them. If your phone or personal alarm is within reach, use it; if not, bang on the wall, floor or radiator to attract attention.
  2. Call 112: If you may have hurt your head, neck, back or hip, or you cannot get up.
  3. Keep warm: Cover yourself with a blanket, towel or dressing gown, and put a pillow under your head. If you are on a hard floor, move onto a carpet if you can, and get out of any draught.
  4. Keep moving: As far as you can, roll from side to side and move your arms and legs.
  5. Have a drink: If there is a drink within reach, take sips.
  6. If you need the toilet: Go if you need to, then roll away from the damp area.

Roll onto your side

Don’t rush. When you feel ready, roll onto your side.

Get onto your hands and knees

Push up with your arms onto your hands and knees.

Crawl to a sturdy piece of furniture

If one is not close by, crawl to a sturdy chair, sofa, bed or the bottom step of the stairs. If you get tired, stop and rest.

Put your hands on the furniture

Put both hands on the seat of the furniture or on the step and take support from it.

Bring your stronger leg forward

Bend the knee of your stronger leg, bring it forward and put that foot flat on the floor.

Push yourself up

Lean forwards and push up slowly through your arms and legs until you are standing; bring your other foot alongside.

Turn slowly and sit down

Holding on to the furniture, turn slowly and sit down.

Rest and tell someone

Sit and rest for a while. Then tell someone that you have fallen and how you feel.

You’re up

Rest for a while and tell your doctor that you fell

Even if you feel fine, a falls assessment helps you prevent further falls. You can check your risk of falling now with the 12-question fall risk check.

Prepare in advance

  • If you live alone, keep your phone or personal alarm with you.
  • Practise these steps while you are well, with someone beside you and ideally with a physiotherapist.
  • Keeping up your leg strength and balance both helps prevent falls and makes getting up from the floor easier.

Measure it too

  • Can you sit down on the floor and get up again?: test your ability to get up from the floor.
  • 4-stage balance test: measure your standing balance.

Frequently asked questions

Is it right to call 112 after a fall?

Yes. Call 112 if you think you may have hurt your head, neck, back or hip, or you cannot get up. It is fine to ask for help after a fall.

Why shouldn’t I get up straight away?

Rushing to get up while still shaken by the fall could make you fall again or make an injury worse. First stay calm, stay where you are for a few minutes, check whether anything hurts and think about whether you can get up.

Why should I keep moving if I can’t get up?

Rolling from side to side and moving your arms and legs keeps you warm, supports your circulation, and reduces stiffness and the risk of pressure sores from staying in one position for a long time.

Why learn how to get up in advance?

The UK national health guideline from NICE (2025) recommends talking with people at risk of falling about what to do after a fall and how to get up from the floor. Trying the steps in advance, with someone beside you, makes it easier to know what to do if you really do fall. In a study that followed 110 people over 90 for a year, 80% of those who fell could not get up without help after at least one fall, and 30% stayed on the floor for an hour or longer; lying on the floor for a long time was linked with serious injury, hospital admission and moving into a care home. Although call alarms were common, they were not used in most of these falls.

I got up and I’m fine. Should I still see a doctor?

Yes, tell your doctor that you fell. A falls assessment may include balance tests, a review of your medicines, an eye test, a heart tracing (ECG) and blood pressure checks lying down and standing up. A home visit to spot hazards and strength and balance exercises may also be recommended.
