Lymphoedema

Lymphoedema is a long-term swelling, usually in an arm or a leg, that develops when lymph fluid cannot drain properly; it is most often seen after cancer treatment. It does not go away completely, but it can be kept under control with compression garments, skin care, movement and specialised massage. Research shows that exercise done properly neither triggers lymphoedema nor makes it worse.

What is it?

The lymphatic system is the network of vessels and glands that collects excess fluid in the body and fights infection. When this system does not work well, fluid builds up in the tissues and swelling develops.

There are two types. Lymphoedema present from birth (primary) is rare. Lymphoedema that develops later (secondary) is much more common: it appears when cancer treatments such as surgical removal of lymph nodes, an infection, an injury, inflammation or a long period of immobility damage the lymphatic system.

Symptoms

  • Swelling in all or part of an arm, a leg or another part of the body
  • Clothes, rings or a watch starting to feel tight
  • A feeling of heaviness and aching, difficulty moving
  • In the early stage, swelling that leaves a dent when pressed, gets worse during the day and eases overnight
  • Hard, thickened or tight skin
  • Repeated skin infections

Does exercise increase the swelling?

For many years, people treated for breast cancer were told not to tire their arms. Research has not borne out this fear. According to the Cochrane review, progressive resistance exercise does not increase the risk of lymphoedema as long as symptoms are closely monitored (two trials, 358 people); starting shoulder movements early after surgery does not appear to increase the risk either, and it improves shoulder movement in the first months. Because the overall quality of the evidence is low, the authors ask for the results to be interpreted with caution.

The American College of Sports Medicine considers supervised, progressive resistance exercise safe for people who have lymphoedema or are at risk of it. Contracting muscles push lymph fluid along the vessels; that is why movement is one of the four main components of treatment.

Three rules: start gently and build up little by little. If you have a compression garment, wear it while exercising. Check the swollen area during and after exercise; if anything changes, stop and let your lymphoedema team know.

Treatment

The recommended treatment is “decongestive lymphatic therapy”, which has four components. First comes an intensive phase of several weeks aimed at reducing the swelling; then the maintenance phase begins, in which you take over your own care and go for a check-up every few months.

  • CompressionBandages and compression garments (sleeves, gloves, stockings) stop the fluid building up again; you are taught the right fit and how to put them on.
  • Skin careKeeping the skin clean, moisturised and intact lowers the risk of infection.
  • Movement and exerciseMovements specific to the affected area, together with whole-body activities such as walking, swimming and cycling.
  • Lymphatic drainage massageCarried out by a specialist therapist at first; in the maintenance phase you are taught simple techniques to do yourself.
  • Elevation and weightSupporting the affected area on a pillow when resting and keeping to a healthy weight are recommended.
  • SurgeryUsed in a small number of people, and only at some specialist centres.

Protecting the skin, preventing infection

In an area with lymphoedema the skin is more open to infection, and every infection can damage the lymphatic system a little more. That is why small precautions matter:

  • Clean cuts and scratches straight away and apply antiseptic cream
  • Moisturise your skin every day; keep it clean and dry
  • Wear gloves for gardening and housework; use an electric razor for shaving and nail clippers for your nails
  • Protect yourself from sunburn and insect bites
  • Avoid very hot baths, saunas and steam rooms; they can increase the swelling
  • Avoid tight clothes and jewellery
  • Where possible, do not have injections or your blood pressure taken in the affected area
  • If your legs are affected, do not go barefoot, and wear shoes that fit well

Exercises at home

Six movements for arm lymphoedema

Sit upright with your arm comfortably supported. The movements should be painless; a slight stretching feeling is normal. Talk to your doctor or lymphoedema therapist before you start. The movements are different for lymphoedema of the leg or of the head and neck.

Deep belly breathing

Relax your shoulders and upper chest. Rest one hand just below your ribs. Breathe in slowly and deeply through your nose, feeling your hand rise as your tummy rises; breathe out slowly through your mouth. Always begin the other movements with this.

5 breaths

Shoulder shrugs and rolls

Sit upright. Raise your shoulders towards your ears, then let them drop. Then roll your shoulders forwards, and then backwards.

5–10 reps

Hands from knees to shoulders

Sit upright with your hands on your knees. Bring your hands to your shoulders, then lift your arms as high as is comfortable and lower them back to your knees. There should be no pain; a slight stretching feeling is normal.

5–10 reps

Making a fist and spreading the fingers

Make a fist, then spread your fingers as wide as you can. Then touch your thumb to the tip of each finger in turn.

5–10 reps

Ball squeeze

Slowly squeeze a soft ball in your palm or between your two hands, and release. Muscle contractions help lymph fluid move along the vessels.

5–10 reps

Walking

If you have been inactive for a long time, walking is the best way to start. Begin with a short distance and build up slowly. If you have a compression garment, wear it while exercising; check the swollen area during and after exercise.

Every day, building up little by little

When sitting, rest your arm on a pillow at a comfortable height. If your leg is affected, do not sit for long with your feet hanging down; put your leg up with a pillow under your knee.

Frequently asked questions

Does lymphoedema go away completely?

There is no cure for lymphoedema; however, symptoms can usually be kept under control with compression garments, skin care, regular movement and specialised massage. Care that starts early helps stop the swelling from progressing.

Does exercise make lymphoedema worse?

Research does not show this. In the two trials in the Cochrane review (358 people), progressive resistance exercise did not increase the risk of lymphoedema as long as symptoms were monitored. Muscle contractions help lymph fluid move along. Start gently, wear your compression garment during exercise if you have one, and check the swollen area.

Can I have my blood pressure taken or blood drawn from the arm with lymphoedema?

The UK’s National Health Service (NHS) advises avoiding injections and blood pressure readings in the affected area where possible. Tell the health professional that you have lymphoedema and ask them to use your other arm.

What should I do if the swelling suddenly increases and the skin turns red?

Redness, warmth, pain, increased swelling, fever or shivering can be signs of a skin infection (cellulitis). Infection develops more easily in an area with lymphoedema and can damage the lymphatic system further; see a doctor the same day, without waiting. Treatment is usually antibiotics by mouth.

When to seek help right away

See a doctor without delay if you have any of the following:

  • Redness, warmth, pain or a sudden increase in swelling in the swollen area; fever or shivering (the same day)
  • Sudden painful swelling in one leg (emergency)
  • Shortness of breath or chest pain (emergency)
  • New swelling of an arm or leg with no known cause
  • If you have had cancer treatment: swelling that increases quickly, or a new lump you can feel
Lymphoedema care is carried out under the supervision of a lymphoedema therapist trained in this field and your doctor. The movements on this page do not replace that care; they support it.
