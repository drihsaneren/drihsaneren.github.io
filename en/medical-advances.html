Nobel 2026October 2026

Light, a mirror and ice: the 2026 Nobel Prizes

The medicine prize went to three pioneers of optogenetics, which lets brain cells be switched on and off with light; the chemistry prize to Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai, who showed how to make only one of two mirror-image molecules; and the physics prize to Francis Halzen, who caught cosmic neutrinos in the ice of the South Pole.

All three began with a curiosity that looked useless at the time: a pond alga, a reaction that did not add up, and a flash of light at the bottom of the ice. I have explained all three in plain language, with animations.

Sources: STAT, 5 October 2026 · IceCube, 6 October 2026 · Forbes, 7 October 2026

Alzheimer'sSeptember 2026

More blood tests for Alzheimer's

Since May 2025, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved four tests that measure amyloid and tau changes in the brain from a blood sample. The latest two arrived in August 2026. These tests may make diagnosis easier, without the need for brain imaging or a lumbar puncture to collect spinal fluid.

Important: the tests are approved only for use in people with memory complaints, as part of an assessment by a doctor. They are not suitable for screening healthy people who have no symptoms.

Source: TIME, 22 September 2026

Organ transplantSeptember 2026

A pig kidney became a bridge to a human kidney

In the US, Tim Andrews, 66, who had end-stage kidney failure, received a kidney from a gene-edited pig. The kidney kept him off dialysis for 271 days, the longest period documented in a living recipient. When his immunosuppressive drugs were reduced because of an infection, the pig kidney was damaged and stopped working; in January 2026 Andrews received a human kidney from a donor, which started working straight away.

Given how long organ waiting lists are, this is a hopeful step. For now, though, experience comes from only a small number of patients; the safety and durability of gene-edited pig kidneys are being studied in clinical trials.

Sources: The Lancet, 9 September 2026 · Mass General Brigham

Huntington'sSeptember 2026

First gene therapy for Huntington's disease awaits approval

Huntington's disease is an inherited, progressive brain disease that affects movement, thinking and mood, and no treatment has been proven to slow its course. AMT-130, developed by uniQure, is delivered in a one-off, MRI-guided operation to the striatum, an area deep in the brain, and aims to reduce production of the faulty huntingtin protein that causes the disease. In patients who received the high dose, the disease progressed 75% more slowly at year 3 than in a comparison group; the company applied for approval in the US and the UK in September 2026.

The results are based on a small number of patients and on a comparison with similar patients selected from disease registries rather than a placebo group. In the year 4 data released on 29 September, the difference on the main rating scale shrank to 44% and was not statistically significant, while a 61% slowing held up on a scale of daily functioning. Of those who received the high dose, 17% had a serious side effect linked to inflammation in the brain; all of these events resolved.

Sources: uniQure, 29 September 2026 · BioPharm International, 2 September 2026

Parkinson’sSeptember 2026

A Parkinson's drug that works in a new way: tavapadon

On 28 September 2026, the FDA approved AbbVie's drug tavapadon (JUVMO). Taken as a tablet once a day, it is the first drug to selectively stimulate D1 and D5, two of the dopamine receptors in the brain; the dopamine agonists used today act mainly on D2 and D3 receptors. It can be used on its own or together with levodopa. In patients taking levodopa, it increased “on” time, the good periods without troublesome involuntary movements, by 1.7 hours a day; with placebo the increase was 0.6 hours.

The most common side effects are nausea, headache, taste disturbance, tiredness and dizziness; care is needed because of the risk of a drop in blood pressure on standing, hallucinations and impulse-control problems such as compulsive gambling. Although new drugs can make movement easier, regular exercise remains a core part of treatment for Parkinson's disease.

Source: AbbVie, 28 September 2026

SleepAugust 2026

First drug to replace the missing brain signal in type 1 narcolepsy

In type 1 narcolepsy, the brain loses the cells that make orexin (hypocretin), a substance that regulates wakefulness. People struggle with irresistible sleep attacks during the day, and their muscles can suddenly go weak when they laugh or get excited (cataplexy). On 5 August 2026, the FDA approved Takeda's drug oveporexton (Orzeyful) as the first drug to stand in for the missing orexin signal. In two studies involving 273 patients, daytime wakefulness improved, and sleepiness and cataplexy attacks fell markedly.

According to the FDA, it is the first drug to act on the underlying biology of the disease. It is taken twice a day; the most common side effects are difficulty sleeping, needing to pass urine often and increased saliva. Its safety and effectiveness in people under 18 are not yet known.

Source: FDA, 5 August 2026

CancerAugust 2026

Pancreatic cancer gets its first RAS-blocking drug

More than 90% of pancreatic cancers carry a mutation in the RAS genes (usually KRAS) that drive tumour growth. RAS was long thought impossible to target with drugs; medicines aimed at just one specific RAS mutation were already used in lung and bowel cancer, while daraxonrasib (Rasonque), which blocks many forms of RAS at once, received FDA approval on 26 August 2026. In the RASolute 302 study of 500 patients whose pancreatic cancer had spread and who had already been treated, median survival was 6.7 months with chemotherapy and 13.2 months with daraxonrasib.

It is taken as a tablet once a day. The most common side effects are rash, diarrhoea, mouth sores and nausea. One oncologist quoted by STAT called it the biggest advance in pancreatic cancer in decades; studies of the drug as first-line treatment are ongoing.

Sources: FDA, 26 August 2026 · STAT

CancerAugust 2026

Personalised mRNA cancer vaccine succeeds in phase III for the first time

Intismeran, developed by Merck and Moderna, is an mRNA vaccine made individually for each patient, based on the mutations in their tumour. In the INTerpath-001 study of 1,137 patients with stage IIB–IV melanoma whose tumour had been completely removed by surgery, the vaccine was given together with an immunotherapy drug (pembrolizumab) and delayed the return of the disease and its spread to distant organs compared with the drug alone.

Detailed figures have not yet been released; the results will be presented at a medical congress. In the earlier phase IIb study, the risk of recurrence or death was 49% lower. No new safety concerns were seen.

Sources: Merck, 19 August 2026 · STAT

Parkinson’sJuly 2026

Stem cell–derived brain cell transplants in Parkinson’s

In the STEM-PD study, led by Lund University in Sweden and the University of Cambridge in the UK, dopamine-producing cells made from stem cells were transplanted into the brains of 8 people with moderate Parkinson’s disease. Of the 7 patients assessed after one year, 6 substantially reduced their medication dose, and brain scans suggested that the transplanted cells had survived.

No serious side effects were linked to the cells. Because this was a small, early-phase study, longer follow-up and larger studies are needed to see whether the effect lasts; the treatment is not yet in routine use.

Sources: Nature Medicine, 9 July 2026 · Parkinson’s UK

Alzheimer'sJuly 2026

A drug “shuttled” into the brain: trontinemab

Trontinemab, developed by Roche, is designed to cross the blood–brain barrier more easily with the help of a carrier. According to early-phase data presented at the AAIC Alzheimer's conference in July 2026, amyloid build-up in the brain fell below the threshold level at 28 weeks in 92% of patients who received the high dose.

The drug is not yet approved; large phase III trials are ongoing. Side effects such as microbleeds in the brain were seen in a small number of patients.

Source: Clinical Trials Arena, 16 July 2026

CholesterolJuly 2026

The first PCSK9 pill for cholesterol

PCSK9 drugs lower 'bad' (LDL) cholesterol powerfully, but until now they have only been available as injections. On 17 July 2026, the FDA approved Merck's drug enlicitide (Lipfendra) as the first PCSK9 pill, taken once a day. In two trials involving a total of 3,207 people already taking statins, LDL cholesterol at week 24 fell by 56% more than with placebo in people with, or at high risk of, cardiovascular disease, and by 59% more in people with familial hypercholesterolaemia.

It is approved for use alongside diet and exercise. The outcome study that will show whether it reduces heart attacks and strokes has not yet been completed.

Source: FDA, 17 July 2026

ImmunologyJune 2026

CAR-T cell therapy for severe lupus

CAR-T, a method used to treat cancer, is also being tried in diseases where the immune system attacks the body’s own tissues. In the CARLYSLE study run by University College London (UCL), immune cells from people with severe lupus were reprogrammed to target the B cells that drive the disease. In the lower-dose group, 5 of 6 patients went into remission by standard criteria, and those with kidney involvement lost less protein in their urine.

There was no severe cytokine release syndrome and no nervous system side effects; liver injury in one patient resolved completely. These results come from an early study of 9 patients; a larger phase II study has started recruiting in the UK.

Source: UCL, 12 June 2026

MusculoskeletalJune 2026

Blocking small arteries for knee osteoarthritis pain (GAE)

At Charité University Hospital in Berlin, 194 patients whose osteoarthritis pain had not improved with other treatments for at least 3 months had genicular artery embolisation: a thin catheter was used to deliver microspheres, which dissolve within a few hours, into small arteries around the knee. Median pain fell from 7 out of 10 to 3 at 12 months, and 80% of patients improved meaningfully.

There were no moderate or severe side effects. However, this was a single-centre study without a control group, and only 79% of patients could be reached at 12 months. Exercise remains the cornerstone of treatment for knee osteoarthritis.

Source: Radiology, 16 June 2026 · ScienceDaily

Cancer screeningJune 2026

Catching many cancers early with a single blood test: the first major results

The NHS-Galleri study in England included 142,942 people aged 50–77 with no symptoms. Half of them had the Galleri test once a year for 3 years; the test looks in the blood for fragments of DNA shed by tumours. In the test group, about 4 times as many cancers were picked up by screening; diagnoses of stage IV cancer (cancer that has spread) fell by 14%, and more cancers were caught at an earlier stage.

The study did not achieve a significant reduction in stage III–IV cancers, which was its main goal. It is not yet known whether earlier diagnosis helps people live longer. This test does not replace breast, cervical and bowel cancer screening.

Sources: ASCO, June 2026 · The ASCO Post

MusculoskeletalJune 2026

New weight-loss drug also eased knee osteoarthritis pain

Retatrutide, developed by Eli Lilly, is a once-weekly injection that stimulates three hormone receptors involved in appetite and metabolism (GLP-1, GIP and glucagon) at the same time. In the TRIUMPH-4 study of 445 people with obesity and knee osteoarthritis, those on the highest dose lost an average of 28.7% of their body weight over 68 weeks; knee pain fell by 74–76% on the two doses, compared with 40.3% in the placebo group. In the TRIUMPH-1 study of 2,339 people, announced in June 2026, pain also fell by up to 73% in participants with knee osteoarthritis, although it is worth remembering that pain also fell by 40% on placebo in TRIUMPH-4.

Retatrutide is not yet approved and is used only in clinical trials; products sold online as “retatrutide” are illegal and cannot be trusted. Nausea, diarrhoea and vomiting were common; at the highest dose, about 18% of patients stopped the drug because of side effects. In knee osteoarthritis, combining weight loss with strengthening exercises remains important.

Sources: Eli Lilly, 11 December 2025 · Eli Lilly, 6 June 2026

Blood pressureMay 2026

A new class of drug for hard-to-control blood pressure

In people whose blood pressure stays high despite taking several medicines, the hormone aldosterone, which makes the body hold on to salt and water, plays an important part. AstraZeneca's drug baxdrostat (Baxfendy) received FDA approval in May 2026 as the first drug to selectively block aldosterone synthase, the enzyme that makes aldosterone. In the BaxHTN study of 796 patients taking at least two blood pressure medicines, systolic blood pressure fell by 8.7–9.8 mmHg more than with placebo at 12 weeks.

The main side effect to watch for is a rise in potassium in the blood: potassium above 6.0 mmol/L occurred in 3% of people on the high dose and 0.4% on placebo, so potassium levels are monitored with blood tests. Do not change your blood pressure medicine without talking to your doctor. Alongside medicines, regular exercise, especially isometric exercise, has also been shown to lower blood pressure.

Sources: New England Journal of Medicine, 2025 · Pharmacy Times, May 2026

ObesityApril 2026

The first GLP-1 weight-loss pill with no food or water restrictions

Until now, GLP-1 drugs have mostly been used as weekly injections, while oral semaglutide (the Wegovy pill), approved in December 2025, has to be taken on an empty stomach. On 1 April 2026, the FDA approved Eli Lilly's orforglipron (Foundayo) as the first GLP-1 pill that can be taken at any time of day, with no food or water restrictions. In the 72-week ATTAIN-1 study, those on the highest dose lost an average of 12.4% of their body weight, compared with 0.9% on placebo.

It is approved for use together with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity. Nausea, constipation and diarrhoea are common, and the warnings about thyroid tumour risk, pancreatitis and gallbladder problems apply to this drug too. Strengthening exercises are important to reduce muscle loss while you lose weight.

Source: Eli Lilly, 1 April 2026

MusculoskeletalApril 2026

Chronic low back pain: which treatment should you start with?

In the US OPTIMIZE study of 749 people with chronic low back pain, participants first received 8 weeks of physiotherapy or cognitive behavioural therapy; those who did not benefit enough switched treatments or moved to a mindfulness programme. At week 10, those who started with physiotherapy had slightly better day-to-day function, while pain levels were similar between the groups.

Although the gains were modest, physiotherapy emerged as a reasonable first step. After one year there was no meaningful difference between the second-stage options; switching or adding treatments later made little difference to the long-term outcome.

Source: Annals of Internal Medicine, 20 April 2026

GeneticsApril 2026

Gene therapy for inherited deafness appears to last

42 people aged 9 months to 32 years with hearing loss caused by a fault in the OTOF gene had a harmless, modified virus carrying a healthy copy of the gene placed in the inner ear. In the study, run at eight centres in China, 90% of participants had better hearing at 2.5 years of follow-up, and more than half reached normal hearing levels. Children progressed from responding to sounds to speaking in short sentences and even singing.

There were no serious side effects. The effect was strongest in people aged 18 and under; 10% of participants did not respond. The treatment is only for OTOF-related hearing loss, and the approval process is just beginning.

Source: Nature, 22 April 2026 · Harvard Gazette

NeurotechnologyMarch 2026

Fast typing at home with a brain–computer interface

In the US BrainGate study, small electrodes were placed in the movement area of the brain of two participants, one with advanced ALS and one with a spinal cord injury at neck level. The system turned attempted finger movements into letters on a keyboard. One participant typed 22 words a minute in their own home with a word error rate of just 1.6%, and about 30 sentences were enough to set the system up.

A big step in communication for people who cannot speak or use their hands. The device is still for research use only and requires brain surgery.

Source: Nature Neuroscience · Brown University, 16 March 2026

Rehabilitation2026

Electrical stimulation after spinal cord injury: hope and reality

In a small study at Brown University in the US, 3 people with complete spinal cord injury had electrodes placed below and above the injury. Stimulation below the injury activated the leg muscles, while stimulation above it gave feedback on knee angle and stepping through signals felt in the chest, arm or back; the participants walked on a treadmill with a harness and a therapist’s support. In the international eWALK study of 48 people, however, stimulation delivered through the skin gave no extra benefit on top of 12 weeks of walking training.

The main message of eWALK: walking training on its own clearly improved walking, even in people who had lived with a spinal cord injury for a long time. Stimulation technologies are promising, but they do not yet replace rehabilitation.

Sources: Brown University, 11 March 2026 · eClinicalMedicine, 2026

Artificial intelligenceJanuary 2026

AI support in mammography: results of the first randomised trial

In the MASAI study of more than 105,000 women in Sweden, mammograms were read either with AI support or in the standard way by two radiologists. In the AI-supported group, 81% of cancers were caught at screening (74% in the standard group); there were 12% fewer cancers found between screening rounds and 27% fewer cancers of aggressive subtypes. False alarm rates were similar in both groups.

AI is not replacing the radiologist but supporting them; interim results had shown a 44% reduction in radiologists’ reading workload. The study was done in a single country, with one device and one software system, and with experienced radiologists.

Sources: The Lancet, 29 January 2026 · EurekAlert!

MusculoskeletalOctober 2025

Changing the way you walk in knee osteoarthritis

In a study of 68 people with osteoarthritis on the inner side of the knee, run by researchers from the University of Utah, New York University and Stanford University in the US, motion analysis was used to decide for each participant whether turning the toes 5 or 10 degrees inwards or outwards would help more, followed by 6 sessions of gait training. Over one year, pain fell by an average of 2.5 points out of 10 (1.3 in the sham training group). MRI findings suggested that cartilage breakdown progressed more slowly.

This is not a one-size-fits-all tip like “turn your toes in”; it needs individual measurement and expert guidance. The measurement will need to be simplified before the method can be widely used.

Source: The Lancet Rheumatology, October 2025

ExerciseJune 2025

Exercise after colon cancer saves lives

In the CHALLENGE study of 889 people with colon cancer in 6 countries who had finished chemotherapy, one group joined a 3-year, coach-supported, personalised exercise programme (from brisk walking to group classes); the other group received health education only. At 5 years, 80% of the exercise group and 74% of the other group were cancer-free; at 8 years, survival was 90% versus 83%.

In the exercise group, the risk of cancer returning, a new cancer or death was 28% lower, and the risk of death 37% lower. It is the first large randomised trial to show that exercise can be part of cancer treatment.

Sources: New England Journal of Medicine, June 2025 · Oncology Central

DiabetesJune 2025

Insulin-producing cells made from stem cells for type 1 diabetes

Zimislecel, developed by Vertex, consists of insulin-producing islet cells grown in the laboratory from stem cells. Of 12 patients who received the full dose, 10 no longer needed insulin injections after one year; all 12 had an HbA1c below 7% and no severe low blood sugar episodes.

Immunosuppressive drugs are needed so that the cells are not rejected. Two patients in the study died, one from an infection linked to these drugs. Phase III studies are under way.

Sources: New England Journal of Medicine, June 2025 · HCPLive

Genetics2025–2026

A gene therapy designed for a single baby

In the US, a baby named KJ had a rare genetic disease (CPS1 deficiency) that causes ammonia to build up in the body. In about six months, researchers developed and delivered a personalised gene-editing treatment that corrected the baby's own mutation.

It is one of the first examples to show that making a treatment “tailored to each patient” is possible for very rare diseases.

Source: Innovative Genomics Institute, 2026

Genetics2026

CRISPR treatments reach the clinic

Casgevy, the first approved CRISPR treatment, is used for sickle cell anaemia and beta thalassaemia. In studies, 16 out of 17 patients with sickle cell disease had no pain crises, and 25 out of 27 patients with thalassaemia did not need blood transfusions. Gene editing carried out directly inside the body is also showing promising results.

Thalassaemia and sickle cell anaemia are common in Turkey, especially in the Mediterranean region. The treatment is expensive and is not available at every centre.

Source: Innovative Genomics Institute, 2026

Frequently asked questions

Can a blood test detect Alzheimer's?

Since May 2025, the US FDA has approved four tests that measure amyloid and tau changes in the brain from a blood sample. However, these tests are approved only for use in people with memory complaints, as part of an assessment by a doctor; they are not suitable for screening healthy people who have no symptoms.

Is trontinemab an approved Alzheimer's drug?

No, it is not yet approved; large phase III trials are ongoing. According to early-phase data, amyloid build-up in the brain fell below the threshold level at 28 weeks in 92% of patients who received the high dose. Side effects such as microbleeds in the brain were seen in a small number of patients.

Will AI read mammograms from now on?

No. In the MASAI study in Sweden, AI was used to support radiologists, not replace them. With this approach, more cancers were caught at screening, cancers found between screening rounds fell by 12%, and the false alarm rate did not rise.

Can pig organs be transplanted into people?

It is still experimental. A gene-edited pig kidney transplanted into a patient in the US kept him off dialysis for 271 days and then became a bridge to a human kidney transplant. The safety and durability of this approach are being studied in clinical trials.

Are GLP-1 weight-loss drugs now available as pills too?

Yes. In the US, oral semaglutide (the Wegovy pill) was approved in December 2025, followed in April 2026 by orforglipron (Foundayo), which can be taken with no food or water restrictions. In a 72-week study, average weight loss with the highest dose of orforglipron was 12.4%. These drugs are used together with diet and physical activity, under a doctor's supervision.

Are blood tests that catch cancer early ready for use?

Not yet. In the NHS-Galleri study of 142,942 people, the test picked up about 4 times as many cancers through screening and reduced stage IV diagnoses by 14%; however, the targeted reduction in stage III–IV cancers was not achieved, and its effect on survival is not yet known. These tests do not replace existing screening programmes.

Is there a stem cell treatment for Parkinson’s disease?

Not as a routine treatment yet. In an early-phase study called STEM-PD, dopamine cells made from stem cells were transplanted into 8 patients; of the 7 assessed after one year, 6 substantially reduced their medication dose, and no serious side effects were linked to the cells. Longer follow-up and larger studies are needed to see whether the effect lasts.

Does changing the way you walk reduce knee osteoarthritis pain?

In a study of 68 people, gait training with an individually chosen foot angle reduced pain by an average of 2.5 points out of 10 over one year; in the sham training group the reduction was 1.3 points. This is not a one-size-fits-all tip; it needs individual measurement and expert guidance.

Can exercise be part of cancer treatment?

In the CHALLENGE study of 889 people who had finished treatment for colon cancer, those who joined a 3-year, coach-supported exercise programme had a 28% lower risk of the cancer returning, a new cancer or death, and a 37% lower risk of death. Talk to your treatment team before starting an exercise programme.

Can thalassaemia and sickle cell anaemia be treated with gene therapy?

Casgevy, the first approved CRISPR treatment, is used for sickle cell anaemia and beta thalassaemia. In studies, 16 out of 17 patients with sickle cell disease had no pain crises, and 25 out of 27 patients with thalassaemia did not need blood transfusions. The treatment is expensive and is not available at every centre.
