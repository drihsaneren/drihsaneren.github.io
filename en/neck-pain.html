Neck pain

A problem most people experience at some point in their lives, usually not due to a serious cause but often recurring. The good news: regular movement and exercise help reduce the pain for most people.

What is it?

Pain in the area from the base of the skull to the shoulders. Usually it cannot be traced to a specific structure; muscles, ligaments and the small joints between the vertebrae are thought to play a part. Medically this is called mechanical or non-specific neck pain: the pain is real, but there is no serious disease behind it.

If the pain spreads into your arm and fingers with numbness, tingling or electric-shock sensations, a nerve root may be involved. We cover this on a separate page: Cervical disc herniation and pinched nerve →

What causes it?

  • Staying in the same position for hours (desk work, looking at a phone for a long time) can cause discomfort; posture alone has not been shown to cause pain
  • Your neck being strained for a long time while you sleep, or a "crick" that comes on with a sudden movement
  • Stress, anxiety and poor sleep: they increase muscle tension and sensitivity to pain
  • Lack of activity and weak shoulder and upper back muscles
  • Signs of wear (osteoarthritis) in the spine become common with age; but they are also present in people without pain and often do not explain the pain.
  • Sudden whiplash-type injuries, such as in a traffic accident

Four types of neck pain

The American Physical Therapy Association's guideline divides neck pain into four groups and recommends treatment accordingly; not every patient fits neatly into one of them.

1

With limited movement

The familiar "stiff neck". The neck will not turn in some directions, and pain increases with movement.

2

With headache

Pain spreads from the neck to the back of the head, sometimes to the forehead, and is set off by neck movements. This is called cervicogenic headache.

3

With pain spreading into the arm

A nerve root is affected. There is pain, numbness, tingling or weakness in the arm.

Cervical disc herniation page →
4

With poor movement control

Mostly seen after a traffic accident. The neck muscles lose some of their coordination and endurance.

How serious is it? Four grades

An international expert group (the Bone and Joint Decade Task Force, 2008) divided neck pain into four grades by severity. Most neck pain is in the first two grades.

I

Pain that interferes little or not at all with daily activities

No signs of a serious structural cause. Extensive tests or invasive treatment are usually not needed.

II

Pain that markedly limits daily activities

No signs of a serious cause. Pain control and getting active early are recommended to prevent long-term limitation.

III

With nerve signs

There are nerve signs such as reduced reflexes or muscle weakness. Further tests may be needed.

Cervical disc herniation and nerve compression →
IV

With signs of a serious cause

There are signs of a serious cause such as a fracture, spinal cord compression, a tumour or a systemic disease. Immediate assessment is needed.

Neck straightening, MRI findings and "bad posture"

Seeing "loss of the neck curve" on an X-ray report worries many people. In a study comparing 107 people, 54 with neck pain and 53 without, there was no difference between the two groups in neck curvature. According to the researchers, such findings in people with neck pain should be considered coincidental; that is, they may not indicate the cause of the pain.

The same applies to "disc bulging" on MRI reports: in a study examining the neck MRIs of 1,211 people without pain, disc bulging was seen in 87.6%; even in their 20s it was present in 73% of men and 78% of women.

There is a similar finding for posture while texting: in a cross-sectional study of 150 young people aged 18–21 in Brazil, no link was found between neck posture while texting and neck pain. The study is small and did not look at the effect of a bent posture over many years. If staying in the same position for a long time is uncomfortable, change position often.

Past to present

A short history of neck pain research

Research over the last 20 years has highlighted the importance of exercise, staying active and treatment matched to the type of pain.

  1. 2008The Bone and Joint Decade Task Force took a fresh look at neck pain: it divided pain into four grades by severity and reported that the pain does not completely resolve in at least half of cases, and that recovery appears to be affected more by psychological factors and work stress than by physical factors. In research, 50–85% of people with neck pain reported neck pain again 1–5 years later.
  2. 2015A Cochrane review of 27 studies found that, in long-lasting neck pain, exercises strengthening the neck, shoulder blade and arm muscles reduced pain by a moderate to large amount immediately after treatment (moderate-quality evidence); no benefit was shown for stretching alone.
  3. 2015A study examining the neck MRIs of 1,211 people without pain found disc bulging in 87.6%; it was common even in people in their 20s.
  4. 2017The guideline of the orthopaedic section of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA) was updated, keeping the four-type classification from 2008; it recommended different exercise and manual therapy for each type and duration.
  5. 2018In a cross-sectional study of 150 young people aged 18–21 in Brazil, no link was found between neck posture while texting and neck pain.
  6. 2021A review comparing six neck pain guidelines in Europe: exercise and manual therapy combined with other treatments were recommended at a moderate level, and information and reassurance at a weak level; on electrotherapy, acupuncture and dry needling, traction and collars the guidelines were inconsistent or inconclusive.
  7. 2024The Global Burden of Disease study estimated that 203 million people worldwide had neck pain in 2020.

Treatment

Most neck pain does not need an X-ray or MRI; the diagnosis is made through an examination. Treatment is planned according to the type of pain and how long it has lasted.

  • Staying activeBed rest and wearing a collar for long periods are not recommended. Carrying on with daily activities as far as pain allows is recommended and may help recovery.
  • ExerciseIt is the foundation of treatment. Exercises strengthening the neck, shoulder blade and arm muscles reduced long-lasting neck pain by a moderate to large amount immediately after treatment (moderate-quality evidence). No benefit was shown for stretching exercises alone.
  • Manual therapyIn European guidelines, mobilisation and manipulation are recommended at a moderate level when combined with other treatments such as exercise; the guidelines are inconsistent about using them alone. The US guideline recommends applying them to the neck and upper back (thoracic) spine according to the type of pain. They should be applied after an examination by a trained physiotherapist.
  • MedicationPainkillers recommended by your doctor can give short-term relief.
  • Stress and sleepWhen pain lasts a long time, managing stress and getting regular sleep are also part of treatment. Take a look at breathing exercises →

Treatment options: what do the guidelines say?

The findings of the guideline of the orthopaedic section of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA, 2017) and a review comparing six neck pain guidelines in Europe (2021) side by side. The letters in brackets are the evidence grades in the US guideline (B moderate, C weak). The two sources do not grade on the same scale: the US guideline grades the level of evidence, while the European review assesses how consistent and high-quality the guidelines are. Recommendations vary by type of pain; treatment is chosen for the individual.

Information and reassurance

Europe (6 guidelines)
Recommended at a weak level, including encouragement to stay active and to exercise.
USA (APTA)
After an accident, returning to usual activities as soon as possible and knowing that recovery is usually expected within the first 2–3 months (B); information and counselling for long-lasting pain spreading to the arm (B) and long-lasting stiffness (C).

Exercise

Europe (6 guidelines)
Recommended at a moderate level.
USA (APTA)
By type: range of motion, shoulder blade and arm strengthening, endurance and coordination exercises (mostly B).

Manual therapy

Europe (6 guidelines)
Recommended at a moderate level when combined with other treatments; inconsistent on its own.
USA (APTA)
Manipulation or mobilisation of the upper back (thoracic) and neck is recommended according to the type and duration of pain (B or C).

Psychological approaches

Europe (6 guidelines)
Recommended at a weak level only in certain groups (mood problems, psychosocial risk factors, or complex, long-lasting pain).
USA (APTA)
For long-lasting pain after an accident, individually tailored, gradually progressed exercise using cognitive behavioural principles (C).

Traction

Europe (6 guidelines)
Inconclusive.
USA (APTA)
For long-lasting pain spreading to the arm, intermittent neck traction together with exercise and manual therapy (B).

TENS and laser

Europe (6 guidelines)
Electrotherapy (TENS and laser were not assessed separately): two of four guidelines advise against it; overall inconclusive.
USA (APTA)
TENS for pain after an accident (C); laser for long-lasting stiffness (B, as part of a combined treatment) and for new pain spreading to the arm (C).

Dry needling and acupuncture

Europe (6 guidelines)
Inconsistent.
USA (APTA)
Dry needling is among the options for long-lasting stiffness as part of a combined treatment (B).

Exercises at home

Six basic exercises for your neck

Do the exercises slowly and with control. A mild stretch is normal, but do not push into sharp pain. If the pain starts to spread into your arm, stop that exercise and talk to your physiotherapist.

Chin tuck

Sit up straight, looking straight ahead. Without tilting your head, draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin. Feel the back of your neck lengthen; hold for 5 seconds, then relax.

10 reps, 2–3 times a day

Neck rotation

Keep your shoulders still. Slowly turn your head to the right and hold for 2–3 seconds at the furthest comfortable point, then turn to the left in the same way.

10 reps in each direction

Side stretch

With your right hand, hold the edge of the chair you are sitting on. Bring your left ear towards your left shoulder. You will feel a gentle stretch on the right side of your neck. Do not lift your shoulder.

Hold for 20–30 seconds, 3 times on each side

Shoulder blade squeeze

Stand or sit up straight. Without lifting your shoulders towards your ears, draw your shoulder blades back and slightly down towards each other. Hold for 5 seconds, then relax.

10–15 reps, twice a day

Isometric strengthening

Place your palm on your forehead. Without moving your head, push gently into your hand while your hand pushes back. Repeat for the back and both sides of your head. Half your strength is enough.

5 seconds in each direction, 5 reps

Upper back (thoracic) extension

Sit on a chair with a low back and clasp your hands behind your neck. Using the top of the backrest as a pivot, open your chest towards the ceiling and gently lean back. Stretch your upper back, not your neck.

5–10 reps

Looking after your neck at your desk

Staying in the same position at a screen for hours can cause neck discomfort. Research has not clearly shown that posture causes neck pain; even so, these small adjustments can help you work comfortably.

  • Have the top edge of the screen at eye level or slightly below.
  • If you work on a laptop for long periods, use a laptop stand and a separate keyboard.
  • Raise your phone to eye level; lower your eyes, not your head.
  • Every half hour, get up, roll your shoulders and walk a few steps.
  • When you are on the phone, do not wedge it between your shoulder and your ear.

Frequently asked questions

How long does neck pain last?

The course varies from person to person. According to an international expert group, neck pain does not completely resolve in at least half of cases; in research, 50–85% of people with neck pain reported pain again 1–5 years later. So it may be helpful to keep doing the exercises after the pain eases.

Should I use heat or cold?

Warmth helps some people with a stiff neck and muscle tension; but research has not shown a clear effect of heat or cold. If you use them, protect your skin with a cloth and do not use them while sleeping. If the pain started after a blow, fall or accident, see a doctor first.

Which pillow should I use?

There is no single best pillow for everyone. Choose a height that keeps your head level with your shoulder when you lie on your side, and does not push your head forwards when you lie on your back. Sleeping on your front can make the pain worse, because it keeps your neck turned to one side for a long time.

Should I wear a neck collar?

Not recommended for ordinary neck pain or for neck strain after an accident (whiplash); the US guideline recommends minimising its use after an accident as well. It can be used for a short time for new pain spreading to the arm. In situations such as a suspected fracture, your doctor decides on a collar.

Do I need an X-ray or an MRI?

For most neck pain, no. Age-related wear and tear is common in people with no pain too, and often does not explain the pain. If you have one of the warning signs below, or the pain has not improved within a few weeks, your doctor will decide.

Can bending over my phone damage my neck?

There is no evidence that bending the neck to look at a phone damages the neck; but there are few studies on this. In a study of 150 young people aged 18–21 in Brazil, no link was found between neck posture while texting and neck pain. The study is small and cross-sectional; the effect of a bent posture over many years was not examined. If staying in the same position for a long time is uncomfortable, take breaks and change position.

Is a straightened neck dangerous?

On its own, usually not. Loss of the neck curve is also seen in people without pain, and research has not shown a consistent link with pain. What matters are your symptoms and the examination findings.

When to seek help right away

Neck pain is usually harmless. However, see a doctor without delay if:

  • The pain started after a traffic accident, a fall or a blow
  • You have loss of strength in your arms or legs, clumsy hands (difficulty doing up buttons) or unsteadiness when walking
  • There has been a change in your bladder or bowel control
  • The pain comes with fever, chills, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer
  • You have severe night pain that keeps getting worse and does not ease with rest
  • If you have a sudden headache, the worst of your life (112)
  • If you have a stiff neck together with a fever (112)
  • If it comes with sudden dizziness, double vision, or difficulty speaking or swallowing (112)
  • Your neck pain comes with chest pain, shortness of breath or sweating (it may be your heart; call 112)
