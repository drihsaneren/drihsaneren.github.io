Scoliosis

A sideways curve of the spine that also twists around its own axis. It most often appears in adolescence, in a form with no known cause (idiopathic), and it is usually painless. Because the curve progresses most during the rapid growth spurt of adolescence, spotting it early matters. In this guide we explain curve types, how curves are measured on X-rays, when curves progress and how treatment has changed over time, showing research findings and clinical experience separately.

What is scoliosis?

Scoliosis is diagnosed when an X-ray shows the spine curving sideways by 10 degrees or more, with rotation of the vertebrae. So scoliosis is not just a sideways bend but a three-dimensional change in shape: as the vertebrae shift sideways they also rotate around their own axis, pushing the ribs backwards on one side. In about 80% of cases no specific cause is found; this is called idiopathic scoliosis. About 30% of adolescents diagnosed with it have a family member with scoliosis.

Small curves are about equally common in girls and boys, but larger, progressive curves are much more common in girls: according to the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS), girls are 5–8 times more likely than boys to have a curve progress far enough to need treatment.

How it differs from poor posture

Poor posture, muscle spasm due to pain or a difference in leg length can also make the spine look tilted to one side. In these functional curves there is no rotation of the vertebrae, and the curve corrects when the cause is removed, for example when standing up straight, sitting or lying down. In true (structural) scoliosis, by contrast, a hump appears on one side of the back when bending forward. According to the SRS, a leg length difference does not cause scoliosis; however, a large difference can make a curve look more pronounced.

How can you spot it?

  • One shoulder or one shoulder blade looking higher than the other
  • The gaps on either side of the waist looking different
  • A bulge on one side of the back when bending forwards (rib hump)
  • Clothes, a skirt or trousers hanging to one side
  • Adolescent scoliosis is usually painless, so it can easily go unnoticed

C and S curves

On an X-ray taken from behind, a spine with scoliosis often looks like the letter C or S. A C curve has a single arc. An S curve has two arcs facing opposite directions, one in the chest region and one in the lower back; one may be the main (structural) curve and the other a secondary curve that develops to balance the trunk, or both may be structural. Curves are named by where their apex lies: thoracic (chest), thoracolumbar (where the chest meets the lower back) and lumbar (lower back); curves in the chest region most often point to the right.

The drawings are simplified to show curve types; they are not real X-ray images.

When planning surgery, surgeons classify curves in more detail (for example with the Lenke classification); physiotherapists likewise choose scoliosis-specific exercises according to the curve type. So even two curves of the same size may need different programmes.

X-rays and the Cobb angle

The size of scoliosis is measured with the Cobb angle on a standing full-spine X-ray. At each end of the curve, the vertebrae most tilted towards each other (the end vertebrae) are chosen; the angle between lines drawn along the upper surface of the upper end vertebra and the lower surface of the lower end vertebra is the size of the curve. A curve of 10 degrees or more is considered scoliosis.

The measurement has a margin of error: different measurements of the same X-ray can differ by 5–10 degrees, and the angle can vary by about 5 degrees over the course of a day. So a change of a few degrees does not on its own mean progression; decisions are based on measurements over time together with the examination.

  • Under 25 degreesIn growing children, usually close observation. The international SOSORT guideline also recommends scoliosis-specific exercises at this stage; the SRS, however, states that the treatments shown to change the course of the curve are bracing and surgery.
  • 20–25 to 40–45 degreesIn growing children a brace is usually recommended; between 20 and 25 degrees, the choice between observation and bracing depends on whether the curve is progressing and on remaining growth. SOSORT recommends continuing exercises alongside the brace.
  • Over 45–50 degreesIn children who are still growing, surgery is considered. The threshold is lower for lumbar curves (35–40 degrees); curves beyond these limits tend to keep progressing into adulthood.

The degree thresholds are taken from the SRS patient handbook and the exercise recommendations from the SOSORT guideline; the final decision rests with your doctor.

Checking at home and measuring remaining growth

In school screening, trunk rotation is measured with a scoliometer; in a study of 966 students in Portugal, 7 degrees or more was taken as marked rotation and was found in 4.2% of students. The Risser sign is read from the rim of the pelvis on an X-ray and is used to estimate how much growth remains.

Types and causes

1

Idiopathic

The most common type (about 80%). By age at onset it is divided into infantile (0–3 years), juvenile (4–9 years) and adolescent (from age 10 until growth ends); the adolescent type is the most common.

2

Congenital

Caused by vertebrae that formed incompletely or joined together before birth; usually noticed at an early age.

3

Neuromuscular and syndromic

Can develop from muscle imbalance in conditions such as cerebral palsy or muscle diseases, or from tissue laxity in connective tissue disorders such as Marfan or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

4

Adult type

A curve that began in adolescence and progresses slowly in adulthood, or scoliosis that appears later in life from wear of the discs and joints (degenerative).

When does a curve progress?

Curves progress most during the rapid growth spurt of adolescence. The peak of the growth spurt comes earlier in girls than in boys; in girls, the period before the first period is the riskiest. So in young people going through the growth spurt (according to the SRS, usually between ages 10 and 15) a curve can increase even within a few months.

  • 727 childrenIn a classic study that followed untreated children with curves of 5–29 degrees until the end of growth, the curve progressed in 23%. Progression was best predicted by the size of the curve, age, the Risser sign and whether menstruation had started.
  • 68% vs 23%For curves of 20–29 degrees, the chance of progression was reported as 68% in children with most of their growth still ahead (Risser 0–1) and about 23% in those further along in growth (Risser 2–4).
  • 83% vs 4%In this study of girls treated with a brace, 50 of the 60 girls (83%) whose curve exceeded 30 degrees at the peak of the growth spurt progressed to 45 degrees or more; of the 28 girls with curves of 30 degrees or less, only 1 (4%) did. In 90% of the children, growth stopped within 3.6 years after the peak.

After growth ends, small curves usually progress little, while curves over 50 degrees usually continue to progress slowly in adulthood. In a Danish study in which patients treated with a brace or observation were followed for an average of 41 years, curves increased by an average of 0.2–0.5 degrees per year; even among curves under 25 degrees, some patients had an increase of more than 10 degrees, and the differences between individuals were large. In an approximately 50-year follow-up of untreated patients, shortness of breath was more common especially with very large curves (over 80 degrees) in the chest region.

One-sided habits and connective tissue flexibility

By one-sided habits we mean not only carrying a bag on one shoulder, but any habit that loads the trunk towards the same side for long periods every day. The ones we see most often in school-age children:

  • Always sitting at the desk leaning to the same side, against a wall or on one elbow
  • Sitting with the weight always on the same buttock, or always with one leg tucked underneath
  • Shifting the trunk to one side and dropping one shoulder while writing
  • Sitting in the same place in the classroom all year and always turning the same way to see the board
  • Always standing with the weight on the same leg
  • Always carrying a bag on one shoulder, or a heavy item or musical instrument on the same side

Families ask about this more than almost anything else. We set out what research shows and what we see in the clinic separately.

What does the research say?

  • According to the SRS, heavy bags, poor posture and sport do not cause scoliosis or make a curve worse; however, a heavy bag can cause back pain.
  • In 966 students aged 10–16 in Portugal, bag weight and postural habits were not linked to scoliosis; poor sitting and standing habits, however, were linked to low back pain. This is a cross-sectional study; it does not show cause and effect, and scoliosis was assessed only with a scoliometer, not confirmed by X-ray.
  • In a case-control study of 2,538 students (mean age 13) taking part in school screening in China, suspected scoliosis on screening was 31% less common in those who said they sat upright and 28% less common in those who regularly changed where they sat in class, while it was 40% more common in those whose school desk was too low for their height. No significant link was found with sitting leaning to the left or right. The habits were self-reported by the students, and scoliosis was not confirmed by X-ray.
  • In another study in China in which 90,635 students were screened, among the 1,458 students who had an X-ray, those with a habit of leaning or tilting to one side had about twice the likelihood of scoliosis, and those who sat slumped over the desk about 1.5 times; no link was found with the way the bag was carried. This is also a cross-sectional study: it is possible that a child with a curve already leans to one side.
  • In one study, generalised joint hypermobility (excessive joint mobility due to connective tissue laxity) was present in 51.4% of 70 children with idiopathic scoliosis and in 19% of their healthy peers; joint hypermobility was not related to the size of the curve. This is a case-control study; it does not show that joint hypermobility causes scoliosis. The authors recommend assessing these children for joint hypermobility and taking it into account when planning physiotherapy.
  • Among 100 girls doing rhythmic gymnastics, scoliosis was found in 12%, compared with 1.1% of their peers. The authors described the combination of joint laxity, delayed puberty and repeated one-sided loading of the growing spine as a “dangerous triad”. This too was an observational study.

In short: It has not been shown that one-sided habits cause scoliosis. Some studies found links with certain sitting habits; however, these links may also arise because the curve itself changes posture. Poor sitting and standing habits, on the other hand, have been linked to low back pain.

What do we see in the clinic?

From clinical experience

In young people going through a growth spurt, one-sided habits such as always sitting at the desk leaning to the same side or against a wall, always putting the weight on the same buttock, or always carrying a bag on the same shoulder or a heavy item or musical instrument on the same side can make postural asymmetry more noticeable. When connective tissue flexibility is also present, as in benign joint hypermobility syndrome (now called hypermobility spectrum disorder), we see that these asymmetries and back pain can be more marked. This does not mean that one-sided habits cause structural scoliosis; but it is a good reason to share the load between both sides during the growth years.

This is based on clinical experience, not on research findings; it may not apply to everyone.

  • Try to sit at the desk with the weight evenly on both buttocks and the back supported; during long lessons and homework, get up and move around from time to time.
  • The desk and chair should suit the child's height: feet flat on the floor and elbows resting comfortably on the desk.
  • If possible, change where the child sits in class during the term, so they do not always turn the same way to see the board.
  • Carry the bag on both shoulders using both straps; keep its weight under 10–15% of body weight, with heavy items close to the back.
  • If your child does a one-sided sport or activity, there is no need to stop; balancing exercises that also work the other side can be added.
  • In children with very flexible connective tissue, strengthening and posture control come before stretching.

Past to present

A short history of scoliosis treatment

Scoliosis treatment has come a long way, from stretching tables to computer-designed braces and surgery that guides growth. Some methods were tried and abandoned; bracing and exercise have also been tested in randomised trials over the last 30 years, while surgical methods rely mostly on observational studies.

  1. AntiquityHippocrates used a table that worked by stretching and pressing on the spine.
  2. 1911The first spinal fusion operation was performed (initially in spinal tuberculosis).
  3. 1921Katharina Schroth, observing how her own scoliotic trunk changed with breathing, developed a three-dimensional exercise method based on breathing and posture.
  4. 1945The Milwaukee brace came into use.
  5. 1950The Risser cast came into use; the Risser sign, which shows remaining growth, was published in 1958.
  6. 1960Harrington rods began the era of correction with metal implants.
  7. 1972The Boston brace, which reaches only to the armpits and is less visible, was developed.
  8. 1980sElectrical stimulation of the muscles was tried; in a multicentre study presented in 1993, progression stopped in about 80% of girls wearing a brace, whereas in the electrical stimulation and observation-only groups the curve increased by 6 degrees or more over 5 years in about 70%. The method was abandoned.
  9. 1983The Cotrel-Dubousset system was used for the first time, and three-dimensional correction of the spine became widespread in the following years; in the 1990s, pedicle screws began to be used in the thoracic vertebrae.
  10. 2013The BrAIST study, made up of randomised and preference groups, showed that bracing reduced progression of the curve to 50 degrees, the accepted threshold for surgery.
  11. 2016–2018The international SOSORT guideline was updated; scoliosis-specific physiotherapy exercises and bracing were included as the main options for non-surgical treatment during growth.
  12. 2019In the USA, a vertebral body tethering device, which aims to correct the curve in growing children without fusing the spine, received limited approval under a Humanitarian Device Exemption (HDE), based on probable benefit rather than proven effectiveness.
  13. 2024The Cochrane review of exercise was updated; the CONTRAIS trial in Sweden showed that a night-time brace can reduce progression in moderate curves.

Treatment options: for whom, for what, and what does the evidence say?

According to the guideline of the International Society on Scoliosis Orthopaedic and Rehabilitation Treatment (SOSORT), the aim of treatment during growth is to prevent or limit progression of the curve. Treatment is planned according to the curve angle, the curve type, remaining growth, the appearance of the trunk and the person's preferences.

Observation

For whom
Small curves; curves that have stopped growing and are not progressing.
Aim
To spot progression in time.
Caution
Check-ups should be frequent during the growth spurt.

Scoliosis-specific exercise

For whom
Small curves (below about 25 degrees); in moderate curves, alongside a brace rather than on its own.
Aim
To limit progression and improve posture and the appearance of the trunk.
Evidence
Low or very low certainty; some studies show benefit, others no difference from general exercise.

Night-time brace

For whom
Growing young people with moderate curves (25–40 degrees); the doctor decides on the type of brace.
Aim
To stop progression with a brace worn only during sleep.
Evidence
In CONTRAIS, success in preventing progression of more than 6 degrees was 76%, and 53% with physical activity alone. This rate cannot be compared with the success rate in BrAIST: the definitions of success and the patient groups differ, and the night brace was not compared directly with a full-time brace.

Full-time brace

For whom
Those who are still growing, with curves between 20–25 and 40–45 degrees.
Aim
To prevent the curve reaching the surgical threshold; it does not straighten the curve.
Evidence
In BrAIST, the curve did not reach 50 degrees in 72% of those wearing a brace and 48% of those observed (75% versus 42% in the randomised group alone). The brace was prescribed for at least 18 hours a day; in those who actually wore it for an average of 13 hours or more a day, success was 90–93%.
Caution
It has to be worn for long hours; skin problems and a psychological burden can occur.

Fusion surgery

For whom
Progressive large curves; cases where bracing has not been enough.
Aim
To partly correct the curve and stabilise it by fusing the vertebrae.
Caution
The most common operation; correction is usually partial. Movement is lost in the fused segment; there are risks of infection, bleeding and, rarely, nerve damage, and further surgery may be needed later.

Vertebral body tethering

For whom
Selected young people who are still growing.
Aim
To correct the curve without fusion by guiding growth.
Evidence
Of the 57 patients on whom the approval was based, 43 avoided fusion at 2 years; problems such as cord breakage and overcorrection were reported. The data are short-term and uncontrolled; long-term results are not yet known.

Physiotherapy scoliosis-specific exercises

These exercises are based on three main principles: correcting the trunk yourself in three dimensions, holding the corrected posture, and carrying this posture into daily life (sitting, carrying a bag, studying). Patient education goes alongside them. There are seven well-known schools worldwide: Lyon (France), Schroth (Germany), SEAS (Italy), Barcelona (BSPTS, Spain), Dobomed and FITS (Poland) and Side Shift (UK). In the Schroth method, breathing directed according to the direction of rotation is also part of the programme.

The evidence is mixed. In a study of 45 adolescents in Türkiye, the group doing Schroth exercises in the clinic with a physiotherapist had a curve angle about 2.5 degrees smaller than the non-exercising group at 24 weeks (less than the measurement error), and their rib hump and waist asymmetry improved; the results of the group exercising alone at home were similar to those of the non-exercising group. The study is small and short. In the 2024 Cochrane review of 13 studies and 583 adolescents, the certainty of the evidence was mostly low or very low: compared with general exercise, scoliosis-specific exercises probably reduce trunk rotation (moderate certainty) and, when combined with a brace, may slightly reduce progression; differences in the angle stayed within the measurement error. In the CONTRAIS study of 135 adolescents in Sweden, scoliosis-specific exercise was no better than physical activity alone for moderate curves (success 58% versus 53%).

From clinical experience

Those who benefit most from exercise are young people who keep up the programme regularly and manage to carry the corrected posture into daily life. The benefit is seen not only in the angle but also in postural awareness, pain and the young person's confidence in their own body. When a curve needs a brace, exercise does not replace it.

This is based on clinical experience, not on research findings; it may not apply to everyone.

Brace

A brace does not straighten the curve; it aims to stop it progressing until growth ends. Today mostly rigid braces that reach to the armpits (such as the Boston brace) and Chêneau-type braces that apply three-dimensional pressure against the curve are used; night-time braces worn only during sleep are another option. In the BrAIST trial of 383 adolescents in the USA and Canada, the success rate in preventing the curve from reaching 50 degrees, the surgical threshold, was 72% with a brace and 48% with observation alone. The effect depended on how long the brace was worn. In the CONTRAIS trial in Sweden, a night-time brace was more successful than physical activity alone at preventing progression of more than 6 degrees in moderate curves (76% vs 53%).

Surgery, trunk balance and large curves

Surgery is usually considered when non-surgical treatment has not been enough. The most common criteria are a curve of more than 45 degrees in young people who are still growing, more than 40–50 degrees in people who have finished growing, and progression. Pain that does not respond to non-surgical treatment, effects on lung function and appearance also play a part in the decision. The most common operation is fusion, in which the spine is corrected with screws and rods and the vertebrae are fused.

A high angle alone does not mean surgery. In 117 patients in the USA followed untreated for about 50 years, back pain was more common than in their peers, but their ability to carry out daily activities did not differ and their death rate was not increased; shortness of breath was more common especially with curves over 80 degrees in the chest region. Because curves over 50 degrees at the end of growth usually continue to progress slowly in adulthood, regular X-ray checks are needed even when surgery is not recommended. So the decision is made together with a spine surgeon, based on whether the curve is progressing, whether the trunk is balanced, pain, lung function and the person's expectations.

From clinical experience

In some patients who have finished growing, whose trunk is balanced (the body has compensated for the curve), whose curve is not progressing and whose daily life is not restricted, we see surgeons not recommending or postponing surgery even when the angle is large. For these patients, regular check-ups and strengthening and posture exercises remain the focus. Loss of trunk balance, increasing pain or progression of the curve can change the decision.

This is based on clinical experience, not on research findings; it may not apply to everyone.

At home

A home check and general exercises

Scoliosis exercises should be tailored to the individual. The location, direction, size and flexibility of the curve, remaining growth and pain differ in every young person; one-sided exercises (for example a side plank or stretch weighted to one side) may not work if they are not chosen for the curve type. So plan scoliosis-specific exercises with a physiotherapist trained in these methods, based on the examination and X-rays.

The exercises below, on the other hand, are general exercises that work both sides of the body equally. As long as they do not cause pain, you can usually do them safely; they strengthen the trunk muscles and support posture, but they do not correct the curve on their own and do not replace scoliosis-specific exercises. If you have had spinal surgery or have pain spreading to the leg, talk to your doctor before starting. If pain increases, or if numbness and tingling occur in the arms or legs, stop the exercise and talk to your doctor.

The test on the first card is a simple check you can do at home during the growth years.

Forward bend test (home check)

Have your child bend forward with feet together and knees straight, arms hanging freely. Looking from behind, check whether both sides of the back are at the same height. If there is a clear hump on one side, see a doctor. Even if the test looks normal, talk to your doctor if there is a clear difference in shoulder or waist level.

Every few months during growth

Bird dog

Get on your hands and knees with your back flat. Reach one arm forwards and the opposite leg backwards, without letting your lower back sag. Hold for 5 seconds, then switch sides.

8–10 reps on each side

Side plank on your knees

Lie on your side with your elbow under your shoulder and your knees bent. Lift your hips to make a straight line from your head to your knees, then lower slowly. Do both sides equally; if one side needs more work because of your curve, your physiotherapist will decide.

10–20 seconds, 3 times on each side

Cat–camel

Get on your hands and knees. As you breathe out, round your back up towards the ceiling; as you breathe in, slowly let your lower back sink down. Move slowly and stay within a pain-free range.

10 reps

Bridge

Lie on your back with your knees bent and your feet on the floor. Squeeze your buttocks and slowly lift your hips; hold for 3 seconds, then lower.

10–15 reps

Shoulder blade squeeze

Sit or stand up straight. Without lifting your shoulders, draw your shoulder blades back and down towards each other; hold for 5 seconds, then release.

10–15 reps

Scoliosis in adults

Two types are most common in adults: idiopathic scoliosis that starts in adolescence and progresses slowly, and degenerative scoliosis that develops later in life due to wear of the discs and small joints. According to the SRS, the risk of low back pain with curves under 30 degrees is similar to that of people without scoliosis; with untreated curves over 50–60 degrees, low back pain is more likely.

More detail: Low back pain and Spinal stenosis guides

Don't give up sport

According to the SRS, children with scoliosis can do any sport unless they have back pain. The international guideline also recommends not giving up sport, even during brace treatment, because of its general health and psychological benefits; but no sport replaces treatment on its own. According to the same guideline, contact or highly dynamic sports should be done with caution during bracing; for young people at high risk of progression, talk to your doctor and physiotherapist about competitive sports that move the spine to extremes (for example rhythmic gymnastics). After surgery, your surgeon decides when you can return to sport.

Frequently asked questions

Do one-sided habits, heavy bags or poor posture cause scoliosis?

There is no strong evidence for this: the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS) states that heavy bags and sport do not cause scoliosis or make a curve worse, and that poor posture does not cause scoliosis; a study of 966 students in Portugal also found no link between bag weight or postural habits and scoliosis. However, some studies based on school screening in China found links between scoliosis and sitting leaning to one side or slumped over the desk, and desks unsuited to the child's height; these studies do not show cause and effect. A heavy bag can cause back pain, and poor sitting habits have also been linked to low back pain. In our clinical experience, we see that one-sided habits such as always sitting at the desk leaning to one side or always carrying a bag on the same shoulder can make postural asymmetry and pain more noticeable in young people going through a growth spurt and those with flexible connective tissue; so we recommend sharing the load between both sides. This is a clinical observation, not a research finding.

What is the difference between a C curve and an S curve?

A C curve has a single arc in the spine. An S curve has two arcs facing opposite directions, one in the chest region and one in the lower back. In both, the size of the curve is measured on X-ray with the Cobb angle; treatment decisions take the curve type, the angle and remaining growth into account together.

Can my child with scoliosis do sport?

Yes. According to the SRS, children with scoliosis can do any sport unless they have back pain; the international guideline (SOSORT) also recommends not giving up sport, even during brace treatment, because of its general health and psychological benefits. Contact or highly dynamic sports should be done with caution during bracing; if the risk of progression is high, talk to your doctor about competitive sport. No sport corrects a curve on its own.

Does swimming correct scoliosis?

No. The international guideline advises that sport should not be recommended as a treatment for scoliosis, but that it should not be given up either, because of its general health and psychological benefits. No sport, including swimming, corrects the curve on its own.

Can exercise cure scoliosis completely?

Scoliosis-specific exercises can help prevent progression in small curves and slightly reduce the angle in some patients (differences are often within the measurement error); but they are not expected to remove the curve completely. According to the 2024 Cochrane review of 13 studies, the certainty of the evidence on the benefit of exercise is low or very low. The treatment needed is decided by considering the curve angle and growth status together.

How long should a brace be worn?

The effect of a brace depends on how long it is worn. In the BrAIST study the brace was prescribed for at least 18 hours a day; in those who actually wore it for an average of 13 hours or more a day the success rate was 90–93%, while in those who wore it for less than 6 hours the result was no different from not wearing a brace. Your doctor decides the wearing time. In a Cochrane review of 7 studies and 662 adolescents, a rigid brace increased the success rate at 2 years compared with observation in curves of 20–40 degrees; all the studies showed that bracing prevented curve progression, but the quality of the evidence ranged from low to very low.

My curve is over 50 degrees; do I have to have surgery?

Not always. In growing young people whose curve is progressing beyond 45 degrees, surgery is usually recommended. In people who have finished growing, however, the decision is based not only on the angle but on whether the curve is progressing, trunk balance, pain, lung function and the person's expectations. In our clinical experience, we see that surgeons may recommend regular follow-up for some patients whose trunk is balanced, whose curve is not progressing and whose daily life is not restricted (this is a clinical observation, not a research finding). However, curves over 50 degrees at the end of growth usually continue to progress slowly in adulthood; regular X-ray checks are needed even when surgery is not recommended. You make the decision together with your spine surgeon.

Does scoliosis progress after growth has finished?

Curves under 30 degrees usually either do not progress or progress very slowly. In a Danish study in which patients treated with a brace or observation were followed for an average of 41 years, curves increased by an average of 0.2–0.5 degrees per year in adulthood; even curves under 25 degrees increased by about 7 degrees on average over 40 years, and the differences between individuals were large. The authors recommend that people with a moderate curve at the end of growth have one more X-ray check in early adulthood.

Does scoliosis affect pregnancy?

According to the SRS, pregnancy usually does not increase the curve in a spine that has not been operated on, and scoliosis does not make childbirth more difficult. Ask your obstetrician before starting exercise during pregnancy.

When to seek help right away

See a doctor without delay if you have any of the following:

  • Marked back pain, or back pain that wakes you at night (adolescent scoliosis is usually painless)
  • Numbness or weakness in the arms or legs, walking difficulties
  • Sudden loss of bladder or bowel control, or rapidly increasing weakness in the legs: go to the emergency department the same day
  • A noticeable increase in the curve or hump over a short time
  • A curve noticed before the age of 10
  • Shortness of breath
