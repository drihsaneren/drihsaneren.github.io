6-minute walk test

The 6-minute walk test is one of the most widely used tests in heart and lung disease and in rehabilitation to measure everyday fitness: it looks at how far you walk in 6 minutes at your own pace. This page keeps the time, guides you every minute with standard phrases, calculates your distance by counting your laps and saves your results.

    How to do it

    1. Wear comfortable clothes and walking shoes, and use your medicines and inhalers as usual. Do not do heavy exercise in the 2 hours before the test; if you like, eat something light 1–2 hours beforehand.
    2. Mark two points in a flat, clear corridor. Bring the stick or walking frame you use; have someone with you.
    3. Sit and rest for about 10 minutes before you start.
    4. Press Start and for 6 minutes walk between the two marks, as far as you can at your own pace. Stop and rest if you need to; the time keeps running.
    5. When time is up, stop where you are, add the last partial length and save the result.

    Safety

    • Do not do the test if you have had a heart attack in the past month or your chest pain has recently changed.
    • If your resting pulse is above 120 a minute or your blood pressure above 180/100, talk to your doctor first.
    • Stop straight away if you have chest pain, intolerable breathlessness, leg cramps, staggering, heavy sweating or turn pale.
    • If you use oxygen, use it during the test too, as your doctor has advised.

    This page is for following your own progress; it does not replace the hospital test monitored with oxygen and pulse readings.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I do it at home?

    Yes, but use the result to follow your own progress. The international standard recommends a flat corridor of at least 30 metres, and the expected values for age and height were obtained under these conditions. The result can change as the course gets shorter: in one study people walked about 50 metres further on a 30-metre course than on a 10-metre course. Use the same course every time.

    Why is it recommended to do it twice?

    People usually walk further on their second attempt: in people with lung disease the second test is on average 26 metres longer. So if you are doing it for the first time, you can repeat the test once fully rested and record the better result.

    How big a change is meaningful?

    In chronic lung disease, the change considered meaningful is about 30 metres (25–33 metres). This figure was mostly calculated from group averages; talk to your doctor or physiotherapist when interpreting your own results.

    My distance has dropped. What does it mean?

    In a study following 2,112 people with COPD for a year, a fall of 30 metres or more in 6-minute walk distance was linked to a higher risk of death. If your result has dropped clearly or your breathlessness has increased, talk to your doctor; this alone is not a diagnosis.

    Can I stop during the test?

    Yes. You can slow down, stop and rest, and carry on walking when you feel ready; the time keeps running. The aim is to walk as far as you can in 6 minutes at your own pace; do not run.

    To measure your fitness in a small space, see the 2-minute step test; for related guides, see the COPD and exercise in heart failure pages.
