Teeth clenching and grinding (bruxism)

Teeth clenching and grinding (bruxism) is when the chewing muscles work more than they need to, during sleep or during the day. It affects roughly one in four or five people worldwide, and most people do not notice it. Its effects are not limited to the teeth: the jaw joint, neck muscles, sleep and headaches can also be affected. In this guide we explain bruxism from start to finish, like a lesson.

What is bruxism?

According to the 2018 international consensus, bruxism is chewing-muscle activity that occurs during sleep or while awake, and it has two separate forms. A person can have bruxism without grinding their teeth, simply by clenching or by pushing the lower jaw.

Sleep bruxism

During sleep
Rhythmic or non-rhythmic activity of the chewing muscles during sleep: grinding and clenching. Most people do not notice it; a bed partner may notice the grinding.

Awake bruxism

During the day
Repeated or sustained tooth contact, clenching, or pushing of the lower jaw while awake. It can happen without your noticing during concentration, stress or intense work.

A disease or a behaviour?

According to the consensus, in otherwise healthy people bruxism is not a disease in itself but a behaviour; however, it can be a risk factor for consequences such as tooth wear and chewing-muscle and jaw joint pain. Whether bruxism is present is assessed at three levels:

  • PossibleBased only on the person's own report ("I clench my teeth").
  • ProbableThere are findings on examination (such as tooth wear or tooth marks on the tongue), with or without the person's own report.
  • DefiniteConfirmed by measurement with a device such as a sleep study (polysomnography).

How common is it?

According to a 2024 meta-analysis combining 176 populations, bruxism affects about 22% of people worldwide: sleep bruxism 21%, awake bruxism 23%. In other words, roughly one in four people may clench their teeth during the day. Awake bruxism was reported about twice as often in women as in men (17% vs 8%); no significant difference was found between continents.

How much force acts on the teeth?

Normally the teeth do not touch all day: at rest the lips are closed, the teeth slightly apart and the tongue rests on the roof of the mouth. In clenching, however, the muscles can press the teeth together for hours.

Night-time clenching

J Oral Rehabil, 2001
In a study of 10 people, the peak force measured during sleep averaged 42 kilogram-force per person, and the highest value was 81. In some people the night-time force exceeded the strongest bite they could make on purpose during the day (daytime average in the group: 79 kilogram-force).

Record bite

Guinness World Records
The strongest human bite on record is 442 kilograms, close to half a tonne; it was measured at a college of dentistry in 1986 and lasted about 2 seconds.

Force and wear

Arch Oral Biol, 2011
In a study co-authored by Cihan Hoca, maximum bite force and tooth wear were higher in people who clench their teeth, and the relationship between the two was strong. The researchers wrote that this was the first study to show this relationship.

Summary for everyone

Effects in four areas: teeth, joint, neck, brain

Thinking of teeth clenching only as a dental problem means seeing half the picture. The dashed lines show that the effect can spread like a chain from the jaw to the joint and neck muscles.

  • 1Brain: The centre that starts and regulates bruxism; linked to sleep, stress and headache.
  • 2Joint: The jaw joint just in front of the ear and the chewing muscles around it.
  • 3Teeth: Wear, fractures and cracks; marks on the tongue and cheek.
  • 4Neck: Neck muscles that tighten together with clenching.

1. Brain: where it starts

Bruxism is now regarded as a behaviour mainly regulated by the brain; the bite and the structure of the jaw play only a small role. In a study of patients with sleep bruxism, in 79% of clenching episodes an arousal-like increase in brain waves was seen about 4 seconds before the muscle activity, with an acceleration of heart rate just before it. In other words, clenching during sleep often appears together with a brief lightening of sleep (micro-arousal). Complaints associated with teeth grinding also include disrupted sleep (yours or your bed partner's) and headache. Snoring and pauses in breathing during sleep (sleep apnoea) have also been found to be associated with bruxism.

2. Joint: the jaw joint and the chewing muscles

Chewing muscles that are clenched for long periods tire and ache; pain in the jaw joint, limited mouth opening and a clicking sound can occur. According to the international headache classification, after tooth pain, jaw joint problems are the most common cause of facial pain. A painless click is common and does not need treatment on its own; if there is pain, locking or limited mouth opening, an assessment is needed. For more detail, see the jaw joint disorders guide.

3. Teeth: wear, fractures and marks inside the mouth

Clenching and grinding can lead to tooth wear, sensitivity to hot and cold, broken fillings and teeth, cracks in the teeth and problems with dentures. There are clues inside the mouth too: in a 2026 study of 98 patients in Türkiye, among those with bruxism confirmed by a sleep study, 65.3% had tooth marks on the edges of the tongue and 75.5% had a white line on the inside of the cheek along the level of the teeth. The dentist examines tooth wear and the bite (occlusion); however, the bite is no longer seen as the main cause of bruxism. The signs inside the mouth are described in detail in the tooth marks on the tongue and other signs section below.

4. Neck: the chain continues

It is not only the jaw muscles that work when you clench: in a muscle activity study in healthy people, the activity of the front neck muscles rose markedly during maximum clenching. Jaw joint problems are also more common in people with neck pain: in a 2026 meta-analysis that reviewed nine studies, people with neck pain were about 3.6 times more likely to have a jaw joint problem (very low certainty evidence).

One possible explanation for this link lies in the nervous system: in a group of nerve cells called the trigeminocervical complex, nerves carrying pain signals from the head and nerves from the upper neck converge on the same nerve cells. This convergence is thought to contribute to pain spreading between the head and neck and being felt somewhere else (referred pain). It has been suggested that this mechanism may also contribute to jaw and neck pain occurring together.

My personal clinical observation

During the years I worked with Cihan Hoca, we saw teeth clenching in many of the patients who came to us with neck pain that had not resolved despite previous treatments. Today, too, many people turn to one treatment after another for complaints such as waking up tired, morning headaches, and neck and shoulder pain; in clinic I see that teeth clenching can sometimes turn out to be behind these complaints. In advanced cases that do not settle, I also come across pain that spreads from the neck to the arm and resembles nerve root compression but is not true root compression. You can read what nerve root compression is in the cervical disc herniation guide. I share these as my own clinical observations, not as research findings; not everyone may respond in the same way.

Notice it in yourself

Tooth marks on the tongue, a line on the cheek and other signs

Many people who clench their teeth do not know it; but the mouth and face often give clues. In the 2018 international consensus, the signs looked for on examination for "probable" bruxism include tooth marks on the tongue or lip, a white line on the inside of the cheek, enlargement of the chewing muscles, mechanical tooth wear, cracked teeth, and fillings or dentures that fail again and again.

  • Arrows: During clenching, the tongue is pushed against the teeth.
  • Tongue edge: The inner surfaces of the teeth can leave a row of indentations along both sides of the tongue (scalloped tongue). The drawing is schematic.

Tooth marks on the tongue (scalloped tongue)

A row of indentations along both side edges of the tongue, at the level of the teeth, is called a scalloped tongue. Because the tongue is pushed against the teeth again and again during clenching, the inner surfaces of the teeth can leave marks on the edge of the tongue. In the 2026 study in Türkiye, 65.3% of those with bruxism confirmed by a sleep study had tooth marks on the edges of the tongue, compared with 34.7% of those who only reported clenching themselves.

However, these marks are not specific to clenching. The consensus report notes that marks on the tongue and cheek can also result from ordinary mouth movements such as swallowing. A wide tongue, a habit of pushing the tongue against the teeth and structural differences in the teeth can also cause a scalloped tongue. In a 2025 study of 160 patients who had a full sleep study, most of them referred with suspected sleep apnoea, a scalloped tongue was seen in about half of the patients; those with a scalloped tongue were about 3 times more likely to have severe sleep apnoea. So if you have marks on your tongue and you snore, or someone has told you that your breathing stops during sleep, be sure to tell your physician.

Other signs

A white line on the cheek

Linea alba
A white line on the inside of the cheek, running along the level of the back teeth. It can form from the pressure of the cheeks against the teeth during clenching and grinding. In the 2026 study it was seen in 75.5% of those with confirmed bruxism.

Enlargement of the chewing muscle

Masseter
A chewing muscle that works constantly can thicken over time. If one side is more dominant during grinding, the enlargement may be on one side only.

Signs of wear on the teeth

Wear facets
Flattened wear facets on the teeth and excessive wear of the front teeth; sometimes notches in the part of the tooth near the gum (abfraction). These notches can also have other causes.

Cracked teeth and fillings

Hard tissues
Cracks in the teeth, and fillings and dentures that break or come out again and again.

Morning symptoms

On waking
A headache felt on waking; tiredness, pain or stiffness in the jaw.

Check yourself in the mirror

  • TongueStick out your tongue and look at both side edges: are there wavy indentations at the level of the teeth?
  • CheekLook inside your cheek: is there a white line along the level of your back teeth?
  • MuscleClench your teeth lightly and feel the chewing muscle in your cheeks with both hands: is the muscle hard or tender, and do the two sides feel different?
  • MorningDo you have jaw tiredness or a headache when you wake up? Does your bed partner hear grinding?

This check does not make a diagnosis. If you have one or more of these signs, you can see your dentist, and if you also have jaw and neck pain, a physiotherapist or physician.

Jaw dislocation: if the mouth stays open

The jaw joint can come out of place when the mouth is opened very wide (such as yawning, laughing, singing or vomiting), during dental treatment or after a blow to the face; it most often dislocates forwards. The mouth stays open and will not close; there may be pain in front of the ear, difficulty speaking and drooling. If the dislocation is on one side, the jaw shifts to one side.

What to do?

Emergency
Go to the emergency department straight away. Do not try to force the jaw back into place yourself or let someone close to you do so; before it is put back, it may need to be checked for a fracture.

Putting it back by hand

Done by a physician
In the best-known method, the physician places their thumbs on the lower back teeth and pushes the jaw first downwards and then backwards to put the joint back in place. When needed, medication or sedation is used to reduce pain, anxiety and muscle spasm.

After that

1–3 weeks
Not opening the mouth more than 2 cm, supporting the jaw with the hand when yawning and eating soft foods for between a few days and two weeks are recommended; a check-up with ear, nose and throat or oral and maxillofacial surgery within 2–3 days is recommended.

If it happens again

Recurrence
Having had a dislocation before, excessive mobility of the jaw joint and some conditions affecting connective tissue can increase the risk of dislocation and recurrence. Repeated dislocations can damage the joint ligaments and capsule; in that case a specialist assessment is needed.

Can be seen alongside migraine, fibromyalgia and neck pain

Jaw joint and chewing-muscle problems often occur together with other diagnoses, and their symptoms can overlap. For this reason, in someone being assessed for headache, widespread body pain or neck pain, the jaw joint and chewing muscles should also be examined.

Headache and migraine

ICHD-3; JADA, 2022
The classification has a separate heading: headache attributed to a jaw joint disorder. It is usually in the temples and gets worse with chewing and clenching; it can be confused with tension-type headache. In one meta-analysis, people with migraine were 4–5 times more likely to have a jaw joint problem (low to very low certainty evidence).

Fibromyalgia

J Oral Facial Pain Headache, 2023
In a meta-analysis combining 19 studies, 76.8% of people with fibromyalgia had a jaw joint problem; about a third (32.7%) of those with a jaw joint problem also had fibromyalgia.

Neck pain

J Clin Med, 2026
People with neck pain were about 3.6 times more likely to have a jaw joint problem (very low certainty evidence). The authors recommend assessing the neck and jaw together.

Related guides: headache, fibromyalgia, neck pain.

Why does it happen? Psychological and other factors

Bruxism has no single cause; several factors play a part together. It used to be thought that problems with how the teeth meet were the main cause; today these factors are accepted as playing a small role, and bruxism is regarded as mainly regulated by the brain.

  • Stress and anxietyThe most commonly cited factors. In one systematic review, people with stress were about twice as likely to have bruxism (low certainty evidence).
  • Sleep problemsSnoring and pauses in breathing during sleep (sleep apnoea) are associated with bruxism.
  • MedicationIt can appear as a side effect of some medicines; do not stop your medicine on your own, talk to your physician.
  • HabitsAlcohol, smoking, some recreational drugs and six or more cups of caffeinated drinks (tea, coffee) a day can increase the risk.

Psychological factors: why do they matter?

In stressful or tense moments many people clench their teeth without noticing. Patient leaflets also state that emotional stress can show itself as teeth clenching and that tension can increase jaw pain. According to a review of 45 papers, daytime clenching in particular (awake bruxism) appears to be associated with psychosocial factors and some psychological symptoms; associations with anxiety, stress sensitivity, depression and some personality traits have been reported. For sleep bruxism, however, no evidence supporting a link with psychosocial problems was found. The authors note that most of the data reporting a link are based on clinical examination or people's own reports, while sleep laboratory studies conflict with these findings.

Stress

Most often mentioned
In a systematic review, people with stress were about twice as likely to have bruxism (low certainty of evidence). The studies in the review looked at different types of stress, such as work stress and stressful life events.

Anxiety

Tendency to anxiety
A tendency to anxiety and sensitivity to stress are described as the main psychological factors linked with bruxism, both in children and adolescents and in adults.

Depression

Mood
A link with depression has been reported; anxiety and depression are also common in people with jaw joint pain.

Personality traits

A hypothesis
It has been suggested that people prone to anxiety and negative emotions (neuroticism), with a strong sense of responsibility and anxious expectations, may release emotional tension by clenching or grinding their teeth; this is not yet a proven finding but a hypothesis.

Mood, clenching and pain affect one another

In a meta-analysis combining 20 studies, people with bruxism were about 2.2 times more likely to have a jaw joint disorder, and 2.5 times more likely with awake bruxism. Jaw joint pain is also associated with anxiety and depression. These links may point to a vicious circle: stress can increase clenching, clenching can increase pain, and pain can in turn increase stress. However, these studies do not show the direction of the relationship for certain. This is why treatment addresses not only the teeth and muscles but also mood.

In a study co-authored by Cihan Hoca that examined 273 patients attending a multidisciplinary jaw joint clinic, being female, insufficient social support and muscle-related facial pain increased the likelihood of anxiety or depression. The authors recommended that anxiety and depression be considered alongside muscle and joint problems when planning treatment. For coping with stress, see the what can you do in stressful moments? page.

What can be done psychologically?

  • NoticingObserve in which situations during the day you clench: work, traffic, screen time, arguments or moments of worry.
  • Relaxation and breathingIn a quiet place, put your hands on your belly and breathe slowly and deeply through your nose; practise for about 5 minutes. Once you have learned it, you can use it whenever you feel tense.
  • Regular movement and a sleep routineRegular exercise can help you relax. Improving your sleep by going to bed at the same time every night is also among the steps recommended to reduce teeth grinding.
  • Professional supportIf anxiety, depression or sleep problems are marked, seek psychiatric or psychological support; cognitive behavioural therapy is one of these approaches. According to the authors of a systematic review, approaches that include relaxation training or biofeedback may reduce stress-related muscle activity, especially in awake bruxism; however, the certainty of the evidence is very low.

Night guard: protects the teeth, does not solve the problem

A night guard (mouth guard) is an appliance made by a dentist and usually worn at night. By keeping the teeth apart it helps prevent wear and can reduce the clenching sensation and pain. However, a night guard has not been shown to reduce the clenching behaviour.

Teeth

Cochrane, 2007
A night guard may protect the teeth against wear.

Clenching

Cochrane, 2007; Dent Med Probl, 2023
There is not enough evidence that a night guard reduces sleep bruxism; in reviews, night guards did not show effectiveness in the short term.

Muscles and joint

Result
Because clenching may continue, the load on the muscles and joint can persist; so a night guard is used together with other approaches.

The dentist assesses tooth wear, fractures and cracks, and how the teeth meet (occlusion), and decides whether a night guard is needed and which type.

Multidisciplinary treatment: who does what?

At present no treatment anywhere in the world has been shown to eliminate teeth clenching for good. In reviews, night guards, biofeedback and botulinum toxin did not show definite effectiveness in the short term; although some medicines reduced clenching episodes during sleep, caution is needed because of side effects, and the reliability of these reviews is very low. So the aim is to protect the teeth, joint and muscles, reduce the triggers and ease the symptoms. This often requires several specialties to work together.

Dentist

Teeth and bite
Assessment of wear, fractures and the bite; a night guard if needed.

Psychiatry and psychology

Stress and anxiety
Assessment and treatment if there is anxiety, depression or intense stress; methods such as cognitive behavioural therapy.

Sleep assessment

Snoring and pauses in breathing
If sleep apnoea is suspected, assessment by a physician and a sleep study if needed.

Physiotherapy

Muscles and habits
Awareness and habit change, relaxation, jaw and neck exercises, muscle massage, posture. In a review of 24 studies, muscle pain and activity, mouth opening, anxiety and stress could improve; however, the quality of the evidence was very low.

Medication

Only with a specialist physician's prescription
Limited evidence: a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence in seven small studies. In a study of 20 people, gabapentin reduced night-time muscle activity as much as a night guard; the only publication we could include for pregabalin, from the same group, is a single-patient case report. Do not use them on your own; ask your physician which one is suitable.

Botulinum toxin

Given only by a physician
Injected into the chewing and temple muscles. In a 2025 review of 14 reviews, a reduction in pain was reported in 10, in clenching episodes in 7 and in bite force in 5; however, the reliability of all of them was low or critically low, and no firm conclusion could be drawn.

Which muscles is botulinum toxin injected into?

The schematic drawing below shows the two muscles used for botulinum toxin injections in teeth clenching. The dots are only to show the area; the physician decides the actual injection points and dose.

  • Temple muscle (temporalis): Fans out over the temple, above the ear; it closes the jaw and clenches the teeth.
  • Chewing muscle (masseter): Runs from the cheekbone to the corner of the lower jaw; you can feel it harden in your cheek when you clench your teeth.

What can you do at home?

Noticing it first

Awake bruxism often happens without your noticing. In a study in which 46 dental students reported the state of their jaw at that moment through a smartphone app 15 times a day at random times for one week, the jaw muscles were relaxed in 71.7% of responses. The most common behaviours were the teeth touching (14.5%) and clenching the jaw without the teeth touching (10%). When clenching and grinding of the teeth were added, an awake bruxism behaviour was present in about 28% of moments during the day in these healthy young adults. To observe yourself, you can ask a few times a day: "Are my teeth touching right now?"

  • Rest positionLips closed, teeth slightly apart, tongue on the roof of the mouth. Whenever you notice you are clenching during the day, return to this position.
  • RemindersTo notice clenching during moments of concentration or worry, you can set a phone reminder or put a small note on your desk; the first step in changing a habit is noticing it.
  • Do not tire your jawDo not chew gum; avoid biting your nails, lips, tongue or gums, and do not chew pens; support your jaw with your hand when you yawn.
  • Warm and coldApplying warmth to sore muscles for 10–15 minutes or ice massage for 5–10 minutes can bring relief.
  • Sleep and habitsRegular sleep times; cutting down on caffeine, alcohol and smoking, especially in the evening. Tips for sleep are on the sleep page.
  • RelaxationSlow breathing, regular exercise and mindfulness practice can reduce how much stress shows up in the muscles.

Exercises

Eight exercises for the jaw and neck

Do the movements slowly, within a pain-free range. If pain increases, stop and consult a physiotherapist or physician. Sliding the jaw side to side and pushing it forwards also work the jaw in directions other than opening and closing. If you have recently had a jaw dislocation, do not open your mouth more than 2 cm in the first 1–3 weeks; start the exercises on the advice of your physician or physiotherapist.

Jaw rest position

Keep your lips closed and your teeth slightly apart, with the tip of your tongue resting on the roof of your mouth just behind your upper front teeth. Whenever you notice yourself clenching your teeth during the day, go back to this position.

Check it often throughout the day

Chewing muscle massage

Clench your teeth to find the chewing muscle in your cheek, then relax. Press gently on this muscle with your fingertips, making small circles. Doing it with a warm towel can be soothing.

1–2 minutes, 2–3 times a day

Mouth opening with tongue on the palate

Place the tip of your tongue on the roof of your mouth, behind your upper front teeth. Keeping your tongue there, slowly open and close your mouth. Make sure your jaw moves in a straight line without drifting to the side; it can help to do this in front of a mirror.

6–10 reps, 3 times a day

Resisted mouth opening

Place your fist or two fingers under your chin. Try to open your mouth slightly while gently resisting with your hand; hold for 3–5 seconds, then relax. Using half your strength is enough, and it should not increase your pain.

5–6 reps, twice a day

Sliding the jaw side to side

Part your lips slightly. Slowly slide your lower jaw to the left, hold for 10 seconds and return to the middle; do the same to the right. Make sure the movement comes from your jaw, not your lips, and stay within a pain-free range.

5–10 repetitions, 5–8 times a day

Pushing the lower jaw forwards

Part your lips slightly. Push your jaw forwards so that your lower jaw comes in front of your upper jaw. Hold for 5–10 seconds and return to the resting position. If there is pain or catching, do not force it.

5–10 repetitions, 5–8 times a day

Chin tuck

Sit up straight, looking straight ahead. Without tilting your head, draw your chin straight back, as if making a double chin. Feel the back of your neck lengthen; hold for 5 seconds, then relax.

10 reps, 2–3 times a day

Relaxing breath

Sit comfortably; relax your shoulders and jaw, and keep your teeth from touching. Breathe in calmly through your nose and breathe out slowly. As you breathe out, feel the muscles on both sides of your face and your temples soften.

5–10 breaths, several times a day

Teeth grinding in children

Teeth grinding can also occur in children. It usually appears after the baby teeth or adult teeth first come through and usually stops once the adult teeth have fully developed. If you are concerned, especially if it is affecting your child's sleep, see a physician; if there is tooth wear or pain, a dentist can also assess it.

Past to present

From the Psalms to international consensus

Teeth grinding has been known since ancient times. It was given a name in the 20th century; for many years the bite was considered the main cause. In the last 25 years, however, it has become clear that bruxism is a behaviour mainly regulated by the brain.

  1. Ancient textsTeeth grinding is also mentioned in several verses of the Psalms.
  2. Early 20th centuryViennese dentist Karolyi described clenching and grinding as "traumatic neuralgia".
  3. 1907Marie and Pietkiewicz proposed a French term for teeth grinding.
  4. 1931Frohman coined the term "bruxism", derived from Greek words meaning "to grind the teeth".
  5. 1960s–2007The idea that problems with how the teeth meet (occlusion) were the main cause came to the fore; research and treatment focused on bite adjustments and night guards.
  6. 2001It was shown that before clenching during sleep there is an arousal-like increase in brain waves and an acceleration of heart rate (J Dent Res). In the same year it was written that bruxism is mainly regulated by the brain, with the bite playing only a small role.
  7. 2007Cochrane review: there is not enough evidence that a night guard reduces sleep bruxism; a night guard may protect the teeth against wear.
  8. 2011Study co-authored by Cihan Hoca (Arch Oral Biol): in people who clench their teeth, maximum bite force and tooth wear were higher and strongly related to each other; the researchers wrote that this was the first study to show this relationship.
  9. 2013International experts agreed on a common definition of bruxism.
  10. 2018International consensus: two separate definitions for sleep and awake bruxism; in healthy people not a disease but a behaviour that can have consequences.
  11. 2024Meta-analysis combining 176 populations (J Clin Med): worldwide, sleep bruxism about 21%, awake bruxism 23%.

Frequently asked questions

Will my clenching stop if I wear a night guard?

Usually not. A Cochrane review of five studies did not find enough evidence that a night guard reduces sleep bruxism; a night guard may mainly protect the teeth against wear. Because clenching may continue, the load on the muscles and joint can persist. So a night guard is considered not on its own but together with stress management, changing habits and muscle work. Your dentist decides the type of night guard and when to wear it.

I have tooth marks on the edges of my tongue; does it mean I clench my teeth?

It may. In a 2026 study of 98 patients in Türkiye, 65.3% of those with bruxism confirmed by a sleep study had tooth marks on the edges of the tongue, compared with 34.7% of those who only reported clenching themselves. A white line on the inside of the cheek along the level of the teeth was seen in 75.5% and 42.9% respectively. These signs do not make a diagnosis on their own: marks on the tongue and cheek can also result from ordinary mouth movements such as swallowing. Marks on the tongue may also be related to a wide tongue or structural differences in the teeth, and in one study they were associated with severe sleep apnoea. They are assessed together with a dental examination; if you also snore, tell your physician.

My jaw clicks; is it related to clenching?

A click may be related to the disc inside the jaw joint shifting as the mouth opens and closes. A painless click is common and does not need treatment on its own. If the sound comes with pain, limited mouth opening or catching, it should be assessed. Clenching can be a risk factor for chewing-muscle and jaw joint pain; for more detail, see the jaw joint disorders guide.

Does stress cause teeth clenching?

Stress and anxiety are among the most commonly cited factors. In a systematic review of six studies (three in the meta-analysis), people with stress were about twice as likely to have bruxism (OR 2.07); however, the certainty of the evidence was low. In a study co-authored by Cihan Hoca that examined 273 patients attending a jaw joint clinic, being female, insufficient social support and muscle-related facial pain increased the likelihood of anxiety or depression; the authors recommended that anxiety and depression be considered when planning treatment.

Does a botulinum toxin injection treat teeth clenching?

Botulinum toxin is an injection given only by physicians, and for clenching it is injected into the chewing and temple muscles. In a 2025 overview of 14 systematic reviews, a reduction in pain was reported in 10, in clenching episodes in 7 and in maximum bite force in 5. However, the reliability of all the reviews was low or critically low, and no firm conclusion could be drawn. Your physician decides whether it is suitable.

Do I need medicine for teeth clenching?

Most people do not. A Cochrane review of medicines for sleep bruxism did not find enough evidence in seven small studies that medicines are effective; side effects were seen with some medicines. Gabapentin, a medicine used for nerve pain, reduced night-time chewing-muscle activity as much as a night guard in a small study of 20 people. The only publication we could include for pregabalin, from the same group of medicines as gabapentin, is a case report of a single patient with an anxiety disorder who clenched during the day. These medicines are used only with a specialist physician's prescription and supervision; do not use them on your own. Ask your physician which one is suitable.

My child grinds their teeth at night; what should I do?

Teeth grinding can also occur in children. It usually appears after the baby teeth or adult teeth first come through and usually stops once the adult teeth have fully developed. If you are concerned, especially if it is affecting your child's sleep, see a physician; if there is tooth wear or pain, a dentist can also assess it.

Does teeth clenching go away completely?

At present no treatment anywhere in the world has been shown to eliminate teeth clenching for good. In reviews, night guards, biofeedback and botulinum toxin did not show definite effectiveness in the short term; although some medicines reduced clenching episodes during sleep, caution is needed because of side effects, and the reliability of these reviews is very low. Because bruxism is a behaviour mainly regulated by the brain, the aim is to protect the teeth, joint and muscles, reduce the triggers and ease the symptoms. This often requires several specialties to work together.

When should you seek help?

See a physician or dentist in the following situations; how urgent each is, is shown at the start of each group.

Call 112 immediately:

  • Jaw pain together with chest pain, shortness of breath or sweating
  • Sudden numbness or weakness in the face, or difficulty speaking

Go to the emergency department straight away:

  • The mouth staying open and not closing (jaw dislocation)

See a physician or dentist the same day:

  • The jaw locking: the mouth will not open
  • Swelling of the face or jaw together with a fever
  • Over the age of 50, new temple pain, jaw pain when chewing or talking, or problems with vision

See a physician or dentist within a few days:

  • A broken or cracked tooth, or a broken filling
  • If snoring and pauses in breathing during sleep have been noticed
  • Jaw, face or temple pain that does not settle within a few weeks
