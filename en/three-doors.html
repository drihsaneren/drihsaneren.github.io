Look after yourself · A new approach

Three Doors

Body, breath, mind

Sometimes, however much you think it through, the knot inside won’t loosen. That doesn’t mean you’re doing something wrong: emotions don’t live only in your head – they live in your chest, your shoulders and your breath too. Three Doors is a way of approaching that knot from three directions: moving your body, slowing your breath and putting a name to what you feel.

We have brought together findings from physiotherapy, psychology and brain research with the ideas of masters who left their mark in their fields. At every step we say clearly what is backed by research and what rests only on experience.

    Why three doors?

    Emotions can be reached in three ways

    An emotion is not just a thought; it also lives in the muscles, the breath and words. Behind each door lies a different branch of science and a different level of evidence.

    Door 1 · Body

    Movement may be the quickest way to reach an emotion

    In an analysis combining 158 studies, a single aerobic exercise session moderately increased feelings of being energised and lively; the effect lasted at least half an hour, and the best results came at a gentle pace. In the longer term, a 2024 analysis of 218 studies found that walking or jogging, yoga and strength training reduced symptoms of depression.

    The 90-second march in the session won’t bring these effects on its own; its purpose is to open the door a little. For more, see the Exercise for mental health and Muscle relaxation pages.

    Door 2 · Breath

    A system that runs both on its own and at your will

    Breathing is one of the rare bodily functions that runs by itself but that we can also steer when we choose. In a Stanford study, people who did cyclic sighing for 5 minutes a day for a month saw their positive feelings rise more than those who meditated, and their resting breathing rate fell. In another study, participants who slowed their breathing to about 6 breaths a minute had lower systolic blood pressure under stress.

    These are individual studies; the results are promising but not conclusive. For a longer practice, see 5-minute cyclic sighing breath.

    Door 3 · Mind

    Naming it

    In a brain imaging study of 30 people at UCLA, putting a name to the emotion while looking at angry or frightened faces reduced the response of the area seen as the brain’s alarm system (the amygdala). In a study of 701 people in Finland, each emotion drew its own map in the body. Writing about feelings may help too: in an analysis combining 146 studies, the effect was small but real.

    Most are laboratory findings; their effects in everyday life are still being studied. To track your mood, see Check your mood.

    Our approach

    The five principles of Three Doors

    1. Scientific findingEmotion lives in the bodyEmotions are felt not only in the head but in the chest, the belly and the shoulders. Listening to the body is one way of understanding emotion.
    2. Clinical approachBottom upWhen the mind is very full, it’s hard to think your way out. First the body and breath, then words.
    3. Finding and approachName it, stay with itRather than trying to change a feeling straight away, first notice it, name it and stay with it for a few breaths.
    4. MeasurementLook at your own dataMeasure before and after each session; over time, your own results will show you what helps.
    5. HonestyLabel the evidenceTell apart what is known, what is hoped and what is believed. Look with curiosity, but ask for evidence.

    An honest note: The Three Doors session as a whole has not yet been tested in research; its parts rest separately on different levels of evidence. That’s why we ask you to take your own measurements at the start and end of the session: your own data will show best what helps you.

    Evidence compass

    The body’s memory: from 1895 to today

    From Freud to Jung, from a walk in the park to brain imaging: ideas and methods that have tried to understand the link between body and emotion. Each one carries a label showing how much evidence supports it today.

    Strong evidence

    Shown to work in many studies, or recommended by a health authority.

    Promising

    Early results are good, but studies are few or small; more are needed.

    Debated

    Widely used, but its core idea is debated among scientists.

    Not enough evidence

    It has practitioners, but research has not adequately shown that it works.

    Historical idea

    It shaped today’s thinking, but has not itself been proven.

    1. 1895
      Historical idea

      Repressed emotion, bodily symptoms

      Breuer and Freud suggested that unexplained bodily complaints might be linked to repressed painful memories. Today part of this picture is called functional neurological disorder: the brain is structurally sound, but there is a problem in the way it works. Treatment combines physiotherapy and psychotherapy.

      Breuer and Freud, 1895; Worcestershire NHS

    2. 1907
      Historical idea

      Measuring emotion in the skin

      Jung said words to people, asked them for the first word that came to mind, and meanwhile measured electrical changes in the skin. With emotionally charged words, the needle moved markedly. It was one of psychology’s first experiments measuring the body; this kind of measurement is still used in research today.

      Peterson and Jung, Brain, 1907

    3. 1929
      Strong evidence

      Tensing and releasing the muscles

      Edmund Jacobson set out progressive muscle relaxation in a book. In an analysis of 27 studies of relaxation training for anxiety, the effect was medium to large.

      Jacobson, 1929; Manzoni et al., 2008

    4. 1933
      Historical idea

      Muscular armour

      In Character Analysis and later work, Wilhelm Reich argued that repressed emotions form an “armour” of ongoing muscle tension in the body. The idea has not been scientifically confirmed, but it inspired many body-oriented therapies.

      Reich, Character Analysis, 1933

    5. 1987
      Strong evidence

      A walk in the park

      Francine Shapiro noticed while walking that eye movements eased the weight of her distressing memories. Developed after the first controlled study in 1989, EMDR was included in 2013 among the treatments the World Health Organization recommends for post-traumatic stress disorder.

      Shapiro, 1989; EMDR Institute

    6. 1995
      Debated

      Polyvagal theory

      Stephen Porges proposed a theory linking feeling safe with the vagus nerve. It is very popular in clinical practice, but its core assumptions were criticised in detail in 2023. Some clinicians find the breathing, voice and movement practices it suggests useful even though the theory is debated.

      Porges, 1995; Grossman, 2023; Giroux et al., 2023

    7. 1996
      Not enough evidence

      Spiritual Technology and PEAT

      The community Živorad Mihajlović Slavinski founded in Serbia, and the PEAT method he developed. We found no comparative research showing that it works; we explain below what we took from it.

      Pinal Villanueva, 2024

    8. 2006
      Strong evidence

      A single exercise session

      In an analysis bringing together 158 studies and 13,101 people, even a single exercise session made people feel more lively and energised. The best results came at a gentle pace and in sessions lasting up to 35 minutes.

      Reed and Ones, 2006

    9. 2006
      Promising

      Writing about feelings

      A combined look at 146 studies of people who put their feelings into writing found a small but real benefit.

      Frattaroli, 2006

    10. 2007
      Promising

      Naming it

      In a brain imaging study of 30 people at UCLA, putting a name to the emotion while looking at an angry or frightened face reduced the response of the area seen as the brain’s alarm system (the amygdala).

      Lieberman et al., 2007

    11. 2007
      Not enough evidence

      The Emotion Code

      Bradley Nelson’s method, which claims to find “trapped emotions” with muscle testing and release them with a magnet. We found no research testing the method; the muscle testing it relies on did no better than chance in a double-blind study.

      Schwartz et al., 2014

    12. 2014
      Promising

      The body map of emotions

      In Finland, 701 people coloured in where in their bodies something came alive or faded when they felt different emotions. Each emotion had its own map, and the maps were similar in Western Europe and East Asia.

      Nummenmaa et al., 2014

    13. 2016
      Promising

      Placebo taken knowingly

      In Portugal, 97 people with back pain took pills for 3 weeks knowing they contained no medicine, and their pain fell by about 30%; in those who only continued usual treatment, the fall was about 9%. Expectation and relationship may be a real part of getting better.

      Carvalho et al., 2016

    14. 2017
      Promising

      Somatic Experiencing

      In a study of 63 people, a trauma therapy focused on bodily sensations clearly reduced symptoms of post-traumatic stress over 15 sessions compared with a waiting group. It is a single study and needs to be repeated.

      Brom et al., 2017

    15. 2017
      Promising

      Six breaths a minute

      The group who slowed their breathing to about 6 breaths a minute had lower systolic blood pressure during a stressful mental task that followed, and said they felt better.

      Front Public Health, 2017

    16. 2023
      Promising

      Cyclic sighing

      People who breathed in twice and out slowly for 5 minutes a day for a month saw their positive feelings rise more than those who meditated, and began breathing more slowly at rest.

      Balban et al., 2023

    17. 2024
      Strong evidence

      Exercise and depression

      An analysis of 218 studies and 14,170 people found that walking or jogging, yoga and strength training reduced symptoms of depression; the higher the recommended intensity, the bigger the benefit.

      Noetel et al., 2024

    What we learnt from the masters

    People who left their mark through their books and clinics

    In building this approach, we drew not only on research papers but also on masters who left their mark in their fields, wrote books and worked with people for years. Some of their methods have been tested in research; others have not, or did not pass the test. For each one we say clearly what we took and where the evidence stands. Being included here does not mean we recommend all of their methods.

    Historical idea

    Carl Gustav Jung

    Swiss psychiatrist, 1875–1961

    The feelings and parts of ourselves that we ignore don’t disappear; they wait to be noticed. Jung invited people to talk with the feelings and images of their inner world.

    In Three Doors: the question in the mind door, “If this feeling wanted to tell you something, what would it say?”

    Not enough evidence

    Eugene Gendlin

    American philosopher and psychologist, 1926–2017

    In the method he called “Focusing”, he described how a problem has a vague feeling in the body that hasn’t yet been put into words. Staying patiently with that feeling and finding the word or image that fits it can bring a sense of release in the body.

    In Three Doors: first finding the feeling in the body, then naming it, and the question “If this feeling were a shape or a colour?”. The method has been little researched since the 1980s.

    Promising

    Peter Levine

    American psychologist, founder of Somatic Experiencing

    The weight of difficult experiences can stay in the body. Levine suggests working with it in small pieces, and moving attention back and forth, like a pendulum, between a difficult sensation and a part of the body that feels comfortable.

    In Three Doors: bringing your attention to your feet if staying with a feeling becomes hard. The first comparative study of the method had promising results.

    Not enough evidence

    Živorad Mihajlović Slavinski

    Serbian author, born 1937

    He wrote around 30 books on Western esotericism and developed some twenty methods he called “spiritual technology”. One of them, PEAT, holds that opposite poles lie beneath a problem and that finding and “neutralising” them brings relief.

    In Three Doors: the question in the mind door, “What would the opposite of this feeling be? Can you notice both at once?”. We found no research showing that PEAT works.

    Not enough evidence

    Bradley Nelson

    US chiropractor, founder of The Emotion Code

    He claims that difficult emotions from the past stay in the body as “trapped emotions”. The practitioner looks for them with a muscle test, pressing on the arm, and tries to “release” them with a magnet passed from the forehead to the back of the neck.

    In Three Doors: we share the idea of looking for emotion in the body. But instead of muscle testing or a magnet we use your own awareness, because muscle testing did no better than chance in a double-blind study of 51 people.

    Strong evidence

    Edmund Jacobson

    American physician, 1888–1983

    He showed that the way to notice tension is to tense a muscle on purpose and then let it go, and developed progressive muscle relaxation.

    In Three Doors: squeezing and releasing your shoulders in the body door to notice the difference between tension and relaxation. Detailed guide: Muscle relaxation.

    Strong evidence

    Francine Shapiro

    American psychologist, 1948–2019

    She took something small she noticed on a walk in the park seriously, then put it to the test. EMDR, born from that observation, is today among the recommended treatments for post-traumatic stress disorder.

    In Three Doors: taking an observation seriously, then testing it: the spirit of our approach.

    Historical idea

    Viktor Frankl

    Austrian psychiatrist, 1905–1997

    In Man’s Search for Meaning, written after he survived the concentration camps, he described how, even in situations we cannot change, we can choose our attitude towards them.

    In Three Doors: the question in the mind door, “In this situation, what can’t you change, and what can you choose?”

    Why do some people feel better with a method that has no evidence?

    • Expectation and relationship: In a study of 97 people in Portugal, people with back pain who took pills knowing they contained no medicine saw their pain fall by about 30%; in those who only continued usual treatment, the figure was about 9%. The researchers highlighted the importance of a warm, understanding relationship.
    • Attention and naming: Most of these methods guide a person to notice and name a feeling; that alone may reduce the emotional response.
    • A calm setting: Slow breathing, touch and someone giving you their time can be soothing in themselves.
    • Easing with time: Many difficulties ease on their own over time, and this can be credited to whatever was being done at the time.
    A method feeling good doesn’t show that its explanation is right. But nor does it mean an observation is worthless.

    EMDR, too, began in 1987 with a personal observation on a walk in the park; it was tested in the first comparative study in 1989, and in 2013 it joined the treatments recommended by the World Health Organization. Clinical observation asks the question; science looks for the answer. That is the path of Three Doors too.

    If you are thinking of trying a method that has no evidence: Don’t use it to make a diagnosis, don’t stop the treatment your doctor has recommended, and be wary of practices that promise certain results and charge high fees.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Three Doors a therapy?

    No. Three Doors is a self-care practice you can use in everyday life; it does not replace a diagnosis or treatment. If you have had anxiety, low mood or trauma symptoms for weeks, you can see a mental health professional; for post-traumatic stress disorder there are treatments with proven effectiveness, such as EMDR.

    Which door should I start with?

    The three questions at the start of the session give you a suggestion. In our approach, when the mind is very full, we suggest starting with the body and breath and then moving on to words; but the best door is the one that opens for you. Over time, the measurements at the end of each session will show you which door helps you most.

    What should I do if my feelings become intense during the session?

    You can stop. Open your eyes and look around you, feel your feet on the floor and go back to breathing normally. Coming close to a feeling sometimes makes it sharper at first; you don’t need to push yourself.

    What if I feel dizzy or my balance is poor?

    If you feel dizzy in the breath door, go straight back to breathing normally; the second in-breath can be short and light. If your balance is poor in the body door, you can use the seated option.

    Should I try methods that have no evidence?

    That’s your decision. If you are thinking of trying one, don’t stop the treatment your doctor has recommended, don’t use these methods to make a diagnosis, and you can ask the practitioner, “Has this method been tested in research?” You may want to be wary of practices that promise certain results and charge high fees.

    Getting support matters: If anxiety, low mood or sleep problems have lasted for weeks and aren’t easing, see a doctor or a mental health professional. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, call 112.
