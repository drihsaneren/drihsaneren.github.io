Timed up and go test

How many seconds does it take you to stand up from a chair, walk 3 metres, turn, come back and sit down again? The Timed Up and Go test (TUG) is a simple test used to assess mobility and fall risk. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) considers older adults who take 12 seconds or more to complete it to be at risk of falling. Try it with someone beside you, using the stopwatch below.

    How to do it

    1. Place a standard chair with armrests and mark a line on the floor 3 metres away.
    2. The person sits with their back against the chair, wearing their usual shoes. If they use a walking stick or frame, they keep it with them.
    3. Say “Go” and start the stopwatch. The person stands up, walks to the line at their usual pace, turns, walks back at the same pace and sits down.
    4. Stop the stopwatch as soon as they sit back down. Stay beside them during the test for safety.

    Strength and balance

    Four exercises at home

    Standing up, walking and turning need leg strength and balance. For detailed advice see the falls prevention guide, and for leg strength the 30-second chair stand test.

    Sit-to-stand from a chair

    The test itself is its best exercise. On a sturdy chair without armrests, with your arms crossed over your chest, stand up and sit down at a controlled pace. If this is too hard, start by pushing up with your arms or by using a higher chair.

    8–12 repetitions, 2–3 sets, 2–3 days a week

    Heel raises

    Stand tall, holding lightly onto a worktop. Rise up onto your toes, then lower slowly. When it gets easy, try it on one foot.

    10–15 reps, 2–3 sets

    Heel-to-toe walking

    Walk along the counter or a wall, keeping one hand ready to steady yourself. With each step, place the heel of your front foot just in front of the toes of your back foot, and keep your eyes looking ahead. Follow a straight line, as if walking a tightrope.

    10–15 steps, 2–3 rounds

    Standing on one leg

    Stand upright in front of the kitchen counter, holding on lightly with one or both hands. Lift one foot off the floor by bending the knee back, and keep your balance. As it gets easier, hold on with one hand, then with just your fingertips.

    10–15 seconds, 3–5 times on each foot

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I do the test on my own?

    No. You need someone to time it and to walk beside you for safety.

    Can I do it with a walking stick or frame?

    Yes. Do it in your usual shoes and with the walking aid you use in daily life. To compare over time, just do it the same way each time.

    My time was over 12 seconds; what should I do?

    This is not a diagnosis. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) considers older adults who take 12 seconds or more to be at risk of falling and recommends a more detailed assessment. You can talk to a doctor or physiotherapist and start with the strength and balance exercises below.

    Can this test tell me for certain whether I will fall?

    No. The UK national health guideline from NICE (2025) advises against using a test or scoring tool on its own to predict falls risk; tests like this help to spot problems with walking and balance. Studies in healthy older people living independently also show that this test alone does not predict falls. So if you have fallen in the past year, tell your doctor whatever your result. If you have fallen more than once, were injured in a fall, lost consciousness or could not get up from the floor without help, you can ask for a full falls assessment.

    What if I am younger?

    The comparison values here come from people aged 60 and over. If you are younger, you can record your time and track how it changes.
