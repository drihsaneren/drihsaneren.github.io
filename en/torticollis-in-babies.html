Torticollis in babies

Is your baby’s head always tilted to one side, with their face always turned the other way? This may be congenital muscular torticollis, caused by a muscle on the side of the neck (the sternocleidomastoid) being short or tight. It appears at birth or within the first three months and is relatively common. The good news: with regular stretches, the right handling and play, most babies regain full movement of their neck.

Symptoms

  • The head tilts towards the affected side, while the chin turns to the other side.
  • The baby always looks the same way and finds it hard to keep their head in the middle.
  • They may find it hard to turn their head to one side when breastfeeding.
  • There may be a small swelling on the side of the neck.
  • Flattening of the head on the side they prefer to lie on (plagiocephaly).

What causes it?

  • The baby’s position in the womb; more common in breech babies.
  • A forceps delivery or strain during birth.
  • The baby’s attention always being drawn to the same side, for example by a favourite toy that always sits in the same place in the pram.
  • Sometimes the cause is not known.

Because a head tilt in babies can have other causes, a doctor and a children’s physiotherapist should assess your baby first.

In everyday life: get them looking to the harder side

The examples below are for a baby whose head tilts to the left and who prefers to look to the right (left-sided torticollis). If your baby’s right side is affected, reverse the directions.

1

Interest on the other side

Place toys, sounds and your face where your baby needs to look to the left. Position the cot so that they have to look left to see you.

2

Car seat

Position the car seat so the window is on your baby’s left; they will turn their head left to look towards the light.

3

Carrying over your shoulder

Carry your baby over your right shoulder: they will turn their head left to look around, and your cheek will limit turning to the right.

4

Side-lying play

Playing while lying on their side helps them bring their hands together and develop hand–eye coordination; gravity also gives the neck a gentle stretch.

Stretching and play at home

Four techniques a physiotherapist will show you

A children’s physiotherapist should show you the stretches first. Do them slowly and gently, and repeat them several times a day. Your baby may protest, but they should not be hurt. The examples are for left-sided torticollis.

Side-bending stretch

Lay your baby on their back. Gently hold down the shoulder on the affected side with one hand, cradle their head with the other and slowly bring the ear on the other side towards that shoulder.

Hold for 10–15 seconds, several times a day

Head-turning stretch

With your baby on their back, gently hold the shoulder on the other side steady and slowly turn their head towards the affected side, so they look over that shoulder.

Hold for 10–15 seconds, several times a day

Carrying on your forearm, side-lying

Carry your baby lying on their side, facing away from you, with the ear on the affected side resting on your forearm. Your forearm lifts their head slightly away from the shoulder, giving a side-bending stretch; support their body with your other arm.

While carrying during the day

Tummy time

Give your baby time on their tummy every day while they are awake and you are with them. Put toys and your face on the side they find it harder to turn to; keep their elbows under their shoulders, and you can put a rolled towel under their chest.

Every day, while awake

Frequently asked questions

Does torticollis hurt my baby?

Most babies do not feel pain from torticollis. Your baby may protest during the stretches, but they should not be hurt; keep the movements slow and gentle.

Will it get better, and how long does it take?

With treatment, most babies regain full movement of their neck; the muscle stretches as it grows and is stretched. Without treatment, neck movement may stay permanently limited and surgery may be needed later. If there is no improvement within 3 months of physiotherapy, an orthopaedic assessment may be requested.

What is the swelling in the neck?

There may be a small swelling in the affected muscle. It usually reaches its largest size in the first month, then slowly shrinks. If it does not go away, an ultrasound may be arranged.

The back of my baby’s head has flattened. Is this dangerous?

Torticollis can lead a baby to always lie on the same side and the head to flatten on that side (plagiocephaly). This only affects appearance, causes no other symptoms and does not affect the brain. Your physiotherapist will give further advice if needed.

How should I feed my baby?

If you bottle-feed, hold your baby in the arm that means they have to turn their head towards the harder side. If you breastfeed, keep feeding from both sides and try different holds that encourage turning to that side.

For a sudden stiff neck in adults, see the wry neck guide.

When to contact your physiotherapist or doctor

  • If your baby’s head tilt or preference for looking one way is getting worse
  • If you are finding the stretches hard to do, or your baby seems to be in pain
  • If the swelling in the neck is not getting smaller
  • If you notice marked flattening at the back or side of the head
